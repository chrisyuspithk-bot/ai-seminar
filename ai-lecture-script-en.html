Speaker note: keep sentences short, one idea per slide. The first time a technical term appears, give the English term and then explain it in plain words.

How to use this script (read first)

  1. This script maps page by page to the deck. Each page holds roughly two to three minutes of material. If you are short on time, read only the bolded sentences in each section.
  2. "You" means the whole class. The audience for this lesson is secondary school students, so keep the tone light — it is a class, not a lecture.
  3. This lesson has three show-of-hands votes (slides 37, 38 and 41). You must actually stop and let students raise their hands. Do not simply narrate them. Those three votes are the backbone of the lesson.
  4. The first time a technical term appears, give the English term first and then the plain-language explanation.
  5. If you are running out of time, protect three things: the Russian dolls analogy, the Turing test, and the two winters. Everything else can be compressed.

The lesson has one throughline: AI is not distant science fiction — it is already inside your phone; and what you need to learn is how to direct it, not how to fear it.

Part 1

Opening and Core Concepts (50 min · 01–27)

01

Cover: How Many Times Did You Use AI Today?

Good morning, everyone. I want to open today's lesson with a question.

From the moment you got up this morning to the moment you sat down in this room, how many times have you used AI?

You might think straight away: "AI? I haven't used it today." If that is what you are thinking, then this lesson is for you. Because I am going to show you that you have not just used it — you have used it many times. You simply did not notice.

We will cover three parts today. In part one, we will unpack five terms you hear all the time: AI, machine learning, deep learning, large models, and agents. I will use one analogy that lets you remember how they relate. In part two, we will take a time machine and cover seventy-six years of AI history in one minute, then go back to 1950 to meet a man named Turing. In part three, we will look at a deeply ironic story: how the smartest people in the world made the biggest promise in history, and how they fell the hardest.

You do not need to write code for any of this, and you do not need maths. You only need to hold on to one question: what does any of this have to do with me?

I am not here to turn you into engineers. I am here to make sure you cannot be fooled.

Ops noteBefore you start, check the projector and the sound. Do not rush this slide — you are using this question to build the suspense for the whole lesson. After you ask it, pause for two or three seconds and let it land.

Likely question"Isn't AI a robot?" You can answer: "That is the most common misunderstanding, and we will take it apart properly on slide 10. For now, hold on to this: AI is not necessarily a robot, but a robot needs AI to be smart."

02

Opening survey

Let's do our first survey. I want you to raise your hands — and I want you to be honest.

Question one: since you got up this morning, if you believe you have used AI zero times, raise your hand.

(Pause. Let students raise their hands. Count them.)

Good, I can see a few hands. Remember that, because we are coming back to it.

Question two: if you think you have used it one to five times, raise your hand.

Question three: if you have used it more than five times, or so often you have lost count, raise your hand.

(Pause and look around.)

The most hands went up for question three. So now I have a question: how were you counting? You might be thinking, "I used ChatGPT twice, so that's two." But your answer just now was "more than five" — which means there are other things in your head you did not count.

That is the point I want to make today: most of the time, you have no idea you are using AI. Because the best AI is the AI you never notice.

Ops noteThe whole slide depends on genuine interaction. You must actually pause and wait for hands, and look at the students. If you rush through it yourself, the exercise does nothing.

AsideIf the room is quiet, you can ask students to raise hands first and then invite one or two to say what they used. Do not spend more than three minutes on it.

03

The reveal: 06:30, face unlock

Now let me start taking it apart. I will begin with the earliest moment of your morning.

At 6:30 this morning, your alarm went off, you reached for your phone, looked at it, and it unlocked. Did you type a passcode? No. Did you draw a pattern? No. So how did your phone know it was you?

The answer is: your phone was doing face recognition. And face recognition is artificial intelligence.

So here is the truth: the first time you used AI today happened before you were fully awake. You had not even opened your eyes and AI had already done something for you.

You might say, "That's trivial, it doesn't count." But I want you to notice something: twenty years ago, that was a plot point in science fiction films. Today it is a default feature on your phone, and you think of it as slow — as a flaw.

That is the first characteristic of AI: when it works well, you forget it exists.

Ops noteThis is the first laugh and the first turn in the lesson. When you get to "you had not even opened your eyes and AI had already done something for you", you can lean into it a little — students will laugh.

Likely question"If I unlock with a passcode, does that mean I didn't use AI?" You can answer: "You could say that this one time you didn't, but from the moment your phone turned on it has been making all sorts of AI decisions you cannot see, like battery management and network switching. We will count them on the next slide."

04

A secondary student's morning

Let's count them now. I will assume you are an ordinary secondary student and count from the moment you get up to the moment you get to school.

One: you listen to music on the way. You use Spotify or YouTube Music and it recommends a song you have never heard that fits your taste. You think, "this app is clever." That recommendation is AI. It analysed what you played before, what you skipped, what you replayed.

Two: you watch short videos while you wait. Instagram or TikTok. You meant to watch for five minutes and you watched for half an hour. That feed is also AI. It knows which part makes you stop scrolling, and it gives you more of it.

Three: you check Google Maps before you leave. It tells you, "this road is jammed, take the other one." That prediction is AI. It works from the movement speed of other phones on the road.

Four: you look up an English word. You say to your voice assistant, "Hey Siri, what is photosynthesis." Speech recognition is AI.

Five: you hit a maths question you cannot do. You take a photo and an app solves it. Image recognition plus reasoning, both AI.

Six: before you hand in an essay, you ask ChatGPT to polish it. That one needs no explanation.

I counted six. You used AI six times this morning, and your feeling was, "I haven't used it."

Ops noteDeliver these six quickly, like reading out a list. You can count them on your fingers as you go, to build the sense that the list keeps growing. Then pause for a second and say "I counted six" — that is where it lands best.

Likely question"Isn't that terrifying? It's like being watched." This one matters and you should not dodge it. You can answer: "Your instinct is reasonable. That is exactly why we need AI literacy — not to stop using it, but to know it is there." That answer shows you are not just selling AI.

05

AI is air

Let's pull the opening together.

I counted six things. But the point is not "isn't that frightening." The point is an analogy: AI is not the robot in a science fiction film. AI is air.

What is air like? First, you cannot see it. Second, you do not think about it. Third, you die without it.

AI is the same. You breathe it in deeply every day, but you do not notice it is there. It is only when it disappears — your phone loses signal, your navigation stops working — that you realise: "I actually depend on this."

So here is the first thing I want to tell you today: AI is not a new technology you need to go and learn. AI is an environment you already live inside.

And once something becomes an environment, being able to read it and write it becomes a basic skill — like literacy.

Ops note"AI is air" is the key line of this slide. Write it on the board and leave it there for the whole lesson, because you will come back to it on slide 27.

AsideIf a student asks, "Can't we just not use it?", you can answer: "You can, but you need to know what you are giving up. I am not telling you to use it or not to use it. I am telling you to know." Do not let this turn into a moral lecture.

06

Part divider: opening up AI's real face

That is the opening. Now we enter part one properly.

Part one is called "Opening up AI's real face." In this part we do one thing: we sort out five terms you hear constantly but have never properly understood.

Those five are: artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, large language models, and AI agents. You have definitely heard all five. But if I asked you how they relate, could you answer?

Most people cannot. Because most people assume they are "five different technologies", or "five generations of the same thing, each newer than the last." Neither is right.

Let's start taking it apart on the next slide.

AsideA divider does not need much — thirty seconds to a minute. The point is to tell students where you are going next.

07

Term bombardment

Let's do our second show of hands.

If you have heard of "artificial intelligence", raise your hand. (Every hand goes up.)

If you have heard of "machine learning", raise your hand. (Most hands.)

If you have heard of "deep learning", raise your hand. (Half.)

If you have heard of "large language model" or "LLM", raise your hand. (A few.)

If you have heard of "AI agent", raise your hand. (Very few.)

You can see the trend: the further down the list, the fewer hands. You might think, "so the later terms must be newer and more powerful."

But here is what I want to tell you: these five terms are not competing with each other. They are different layers of the same thing.

Here is an analogy. Have you seen Russian dolls? You open one doll and there is another inside, and you open that one and there is another. They nest inside one another — they are not five dolls standing side by side.

These five terms are five nested dolls. Let's look at the next slide.

Ops noteThis slide creates a gap — you want students to notice "I don't actually understand this." But you must not make them feel embarrassed. Keep the tone light when you count hands: "Fewer hands, that's fine — that's exactly what we're here to fix."

Likely question"Are deep learning and machine learning the same thing?" You can answer: "Not the same — one contains the other. We will take the layers apart on slides 13 to 18. For now, just hold on to the Russian dolls."

08

Key analogy: Russian dolls

Look at this diagram. This is the most important diagram of the lesson and I want you to remember it.

You can see one doll, layer by layer. The outermost layer is "artificial intelligence". Inside that is "machine learning". Inside that is "deep learning". Inside that is "large language models". And the smallest one right at the centre is "AI agents".

Why "nested" rather than "side by side"? Because each layer is a type of the layer outside it.

Here is an analogy. Think about "vehicles." The outermost layer is "vehicle." Inside that is "car." Inside that is "private car." Inside that is "electric private car." Would you ever say, "electric cars and vehicles are two unrelated things"? No — because an electric car is a car, and a car is a vehicle.

These five AI terms work exactly the same way.

So if you ever hear someone say "machine learning isn't AI", you can tell them: machine learning is a type of AI, in the same way a private car is a type of vehicle.

Ops noteThis is the single most important slide of the lesson. Do not rush it. Draw five concentric circles on the board as you talk. The act of drawing makes it stick.

AsideWith a stronger class you can ask on the spot: "Turn it around — is all artificial intelligence machine learning?" The answer is no: the early expert systems were AI but not machine learning. You can leave that until slide 09.

09

The core sentence

Look at this sentence.

"All machine learning is artificial intelligence, but not all artificial intelligence is machine learning."

The structure is "all A is B, but not all B is A." You have met this in school maths: A is a subset of B.

Put plainly: machine learning sits inside artificial intelligence, but artificial intelligence contains other things besides machine learning.

What are those other things? For example the early "expert systems" — where a team of engineers sat down and wrote out every rule by hand. That is artificial intelligence, but it is not machine learning. Because machine learning means "finding the patterns yourself from data", and an expert system is "rules written by a person."

That distinction matters. It represents a shift in thinking: from "people teaching the computer" to "the computer teaching itself." We will go through that in detail on slides 11 to 13.

Ops noteWrite this sentence on the board. You can support it with a gesture: one hand cupped inside the other — the larger hand is AI, the smaller is machine learning. Visual plus movement sticks better.

Likely question"So is deep learning a type of machine learning?" You can answer: "Yes, and the most powerful type. We will cover it on slide 16."

10

Layer 1: AI, humanity's ultimate dream

Let's start with the outermost layer.

Layer one: artificial intelligence, or AI for short.

The definition is simple: any machine or program that shows behaviour resembling human intelligence is what we call AI.

Notice the key phrase: "resembling human intelligence." It is not "doing what a human can do" — it is "showing something like intelligence." That distinction is subtle but it matters, because the question "what counts as intelligence" is the core problem we will tackle in part two.

When did this term appear? The 1950s. That is right — over seventy years ago. So AI is not new. It is a very old dream; it is only in the last dozen years that it has finally started coming true.

Think of it this way: AI is humanity's ultimate dream — we wanted to build something that thinks. From the mechanical figures of ancient Greece to the wooden ox of ancient China, people kept trying. In 1950 we finally had computers, and for the first time the dream became possible.

AsideThis is a definition slide; do not spend long on it. The point is for students to understand that AI is not a new invention — it is a seventy-year-old dream.

Likely question"Does a calculator count as AI?" You can answer: "No. A calculator just follows fixed rules; it does not show anything like intelligence. We will see the difference in a couple of slides."

Ops noteEmphasise "over seventy years ago." It is counter-intuitive and students find it surprising.

11

The early approach: expert systems

Let's look at how early AI was built.

The early approach was called an expert system. The name sounds impressive but the method was simple: a group of experts sat down and wrote out every rule by hand.

The rules took the form: if this, then that.

Here is an example. Suppose we want to build a system that diagnoses colds. The engineers would write:

  • If the patient has a fever and a cough, it is a cold.
  • If the patient has a fever and a runny nose, it is a cold.
  • If the patient has a headache and muscle aches, it may be flu.
  • ...

The more rules you write, the more the system knows. This approach was genuinely popular in the 1970s and 1980s, for medical diagnosis and geological surveying.

Its great advantage is total transparency. Why did it decide that? You open the rule book and see. Even today, in situations that require explanation, that way of thinking still has value.

But it has one fatal weakness. We will cover that on the next slide.

Ops noteWhen you get to "if this, then that", write one or two rules on the board and say "now help me add some rules." When students offer a few, they will run straight into the problem on the next slide.

AsideThis is the old approach; students do not need the details. What matters is that they understand the rules were written by people.

12

Why it breaks down

Look at this example. It is one of my favourites in the whole lesson.

Suppose we want to teach a computer to recognise cats using rules. The engineers work hard and write a pile of them:

  • If the animal has whiskers, it is a cat.
  • If the animal has four legs, it might be a cat.
  • If the animal says "meow", it is a cat.

That looks reasonable. But then...

What if the cat is sitting in a cardboard box? Only its head is showing and you cannot see four legs. The system says: "not a cat."

What if it is a Sphynx, a hairless cat? It has no fur. But a hairless cat is still a cat. The system says: "not a cat."

What if the cat has a sore throat and cannot meow? The system says: "not a cat."

Do you see the problem? The problem is not that there are too few rules. The problem is that the real world varies too much. Write a thousand rules and the real world will produce the thousand-and-first case.

Here is an analogy: an expert system is like a student who only knows how to memorise. Ask it a question straight from the textbook and it answers well. Ask it something slightly different and it crashes.

This is the technical reason behind the first AI winter. We will cover the winters in part three, but you now know why they happened.

Ops notePace out the three counter-examples — box, hairless cat, no meow. Pause after each one and let students react. The hairless cat usually gets a laugh, because they realise how absurd the rules are.

Likely question"Why not just write more rules?" You can answer: "Because the number of rules explodes. And you can never guess what combinations the real world will throw up. That is exactly what the next layer tries to solve."

13

Layer 2: machine learning, from memorising to finding patterns

Now to layer two.

Layer two: machine learning, or ML.

This layer was a revolution in thinking. Instead of teaching it rules, you give it data and let it find the rules itself.

Can you feel the difference? Before, it was "I'll tell you what a cat looks like." Now it is "I'll show you a lot of photos of cats — you work it out."

This is the shift we mentioned earlier: from "people teaching the computer" to "the computer teaching itself."

Here is an example. Picture an amateur Go player and a professional. The amateur relies on memorising openings — that is, rules. The professional relies on having played ten thousand games — that is, experience. Machine learning is about turning the computer into the professional.

Where does the power of this shift lie? In adaptability. You can use it to recognise cats, dogs, handwriting, speech — as long as you have enough data, the same method applies to a huge range of problems.

On the next slide we will use an analogy to make "how it learns" concrete.

Ops noteThis slide is the watershed. Students must clearly feel the shift from rules to data. You can sum it up in one line: "before it was writing a manual; now it is giving examples."

Likely question"How much data is enough?" You can answer: "Good question. More is usually better, but 'more' does not mean 'messy'. We will talk about data quality later. For now: data is the fuel of machine learning."

14

Apples and oranges: you do not read a spec sheet to a baby

This is an analogy I particularly want you to remember.

Suppose you want to teach a one-year-old the difference between an apple and an orange. What do you do?

Would you pull out a spec sheet and read it aloud: "Apple: Rosaceae family, red skin, seven to eight centimetres in diameter, twelve percent sugar"? Of course not. The baby would not understand a word.

So what do you do? You show the baby an apple. You show it an orange. Then another apple, then another orange. You repeat it dozens of times. After a while you show it an apple it has never seen. Does it know? It does.

How did it learn? It did not learn a rule. It generalised a pattern from many examples.

That is the essence of machine learning. Machine learning is not "learning rules" — it is "generalising patterns from examples."

And notice this: the baby has never seen a perfect apple. Some are bruised, some are not red enough, some are oddly shaped. The baby learns anyway. That tolerance for variation is exactly what makes machine learning stronger than expert systems.

Ops noteThis analogy is the key to the slide. You can do it live: pick up two different objects, say "this is a pen, this is a bottle", then hold up a different-coloured pen and ask "what is this?" That demonstration makes "generalising" click instantly.

Likely question"If the baby only ever saw red apples, would it think apples are always red?" That is an excellent question. You can answer: "Yes — and that is the 'bias' problem we will come back to. Your instinct is right."

15

Throw in a hundred thousand cats and let it find the answer

Let's turn the analogy into the actual method.

To teach a computer to recognise cats, what we do is: throw in a hundred thousand photos of cats and a hundred thousand of dogs, and tell the computer, "these are cats, these are dogs — go and find what differs."

The computer finds patterns inside that. It might be the shape of the ears, the position of the eyes, the texture of the fur. The important part is: the patterns it finds are ones a human may not be able to put into words. It will not tell you "I found that cat ears sit at this angle." It just tells you "this one is a cat."

This is where the black box begins. We will come back to that problem.

The same method works in many places:

  • Spam filtering. Not a rule like "if the subject contains 'you have won', it is spam." Instead, show it a hundred thousand spam emails and let it find the features.
  • Netflix recommendations. Not a rule like "if they watched an action film, recommend an action film." Instead, find patterns across hundreds of millions of viewing records.
  • Bank fraud detection. Find the "abnormal" patterns across millions of transactions.

Do you see what they have in common? They all need a lot of data. That is why big data and AI get discussed together.

AsideSpam is the best example here because every student has experience of it. You can ask: "How does your inbox know which emails are spam?" Someone will usually say "it looks for keywords", and you can push back: "What if the scammers avoid those keywords?" That is how they see rules are not enough.

Ops noteWhen you say "patterns a human may not be able to put into words", drop your voice slightly — it is an important setup for explainability and hallucination later.

16

Layer 3: deep learning, a super-factory that mimics the brain

Now to layer three.

Layer three: deep learning.

Definition: deep learning is a type of machine learning, and it takes its inspiration from the neural networks in the human brain.

Notice the relationship: deep learning is a type of machine learning. So machine learning is the bigger circle and deep learning sits inside it — the same nesting as before.

So what is a neural network? Put simply, it is a great many very small units wired together, passing signals along in layers. Each unit does something trivial: take in a number, do a small calculation, pass it on. But when you stack millions of these units together, they can do something very complex.

Here is an analogy: a factory. One production line does something small, passes it to the next, which does something else, and on down the line. At the end, a product comes out.

So what does "deep" mean? "Deep" means many layers. Early neural networks might have had one or two. Deep learning today can have a hundred or a thousand. The more layers, the more complex the combinations it can represent.

That is why deep learning can do things earlier methods could not — recognising faces, hearing speech, playing Go.

Ops noteMake the factory analogy clear. You can sweep your hand from left to right to suggest the signal passing along, giving students a visual.

Likely question"Are neural networks actual neurons?" You can answer: "No. It borrowed the name. Real neurons are far more complex; our models are a very simplified imitation. So do not assume the computer has become a brain."

17

Layer by layer: the journey of a cat photo

Let's follow one photo and see how deep learning recognises a cat.

Step one: you give it a photo of a cat. To the computer, this is not "a cat." It is a large pile of numbers — the colour value of every pixel.

Layer one: edges. The earliest layers only see the most basic things: where there is a line, where there is a patch of colour. At this stage it has no idea what it is looking at.

Layer two: shapes. It combines the lines it found in layer one and starts to recognise "triangle" — a cat's ear. Or "curve" — the arch of a cat's back.

Layer three: parts. It combines triangles and curves and starts to recognise "eye", "whisker", "nose".

The final layer: the judgement. It pulls everything together and concludes: "this is a cat." And it gives you a confidence score, say "ninety-seven percent cat."

This layering up is the central idea of deep learning. It does not do it in one leap; it goes step by step, from simple to complex.

And the most remarkable part: these layers were not designed by an engineer. The computer learned them itself. Nobody told it "layer one should look at edges." It discovered that edges were useful.

Ops noteThis slide works best with the diagram. If the deck has one, point at each layer as you go. If not, draw it live: a few scribbled lines on the left, triangles in the middle, a simple cat outline on the right.

Likely question"What if I give it a photo of a dog?" You can answer: "It gives you the most likely answer based on what it learned. If it learned dogs, it says dog. If it only ever learned cats, it might say 'cat, but with low confidence'. That is why training data has to be broad."

18

What "deep" really means

Let's pull together what deep learning can do.

Deep learning has already surpassed humans at these things:

One, faces. Face unlock on your phone is more accurate than most people. And it does not get tired, and it does not fail because you changed your hairstyle.

Two, hearing. Speech to text and live translation are now accurate enough to be genuinely useful.

Three, Go. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in 2016. Go has more possible positions than there are atoms in the universe. Expert systems could never do it, because you simply cannot write the rules.

Four, reading X-rays. Deep learning models now match specialist doctors on some diagnostic imaging tasks.

But do not misunderstand. "Surpassing humans" means "on one specific task, performing better than the average human." It does not mean it is smarter. A model that is brilliant at faces cannot write you a poem.

This is the difference between narrow AI and general AI. Everything we have today is narrow — it does one thing very well, and only that one thing. That distinction matters and we will come back to it in part two.

Ops noteCover the four examples quickly. The point is the "narrow AI" idea at the end. One line to remember it by: "it is a genius, but a genius who only knows one thing."

Likely question"What is AlphaGo?" Short answer: "An AI that plays Go. It beat the world champion Lee Sedol in 2016. We will see it again on the timeline on slide 30."

19

Layer 4: large models, the super-student who read everything

Now layer four — the one you are most likely to have used.

Layer four: large language models, or LLMs. You may know the most famous one by another name: ChatGPT.

What is this layer? Put simply: we take all the text humanity has produced — articles from the web, books, source code — and pour it in for it to read. It then learns the patterns of language.

What can it do once it has learned that? Ask it for a poem and it writes one. Ask it for code and it writes that. Ask it to translate classical Chinese and it manages. Ask it to imitate Shakespeare for a paragraph and it does that too.

That is why it resembles a super-student who has read everything.

But we said something earlier — you may remember it: it does not go and look things up in a database.

Next slide we will see how big it actually is, and the one after that we will look at the strange fact that it seems to understand.

Ops noteYou can ask here: "Who has used ChatGPT?" and invite one or two to share. This is the layer students have the strongest connection to.

AsideIf a student asks whether DeepSeek, Gemini and Claude are the same layer, you can answer: "Yes, they are all large language models, made by different companies. We will get to that on slide 31."

20

How big is "big"?

Let's talk about how big "big" is. This slide has two numbers.

Number one: the data. The text it read is roughly all the public text on the internet, plus a large pile of books and source code. In books, that could be millions of volumes.

Number two: the parameters. This is a technical term; you can think of it as the number of connections inside it. For today's large models, parameters run from hundreds of billions up to a trillion.

What is a trillion? Here is a comparison. Your brain has roughly eighty-six billion neurons. A large model has on the order of a trillion parameters — more than ten times the neurons in your brain.

But watch out for one misunderstanding: more parameters does not mean smarter. That is a common mistake. More parameters means more capacity to store patterns. But capacity and using it well are two different things. A library with more books is not automatically a library with better readers.

What these numbers really tell you is this: building a model this size needs enormous amounts of computing power, electricity and money. Which is why only a handful of companies can do it. That is the barrier to entry in this industry.

Ops noteThe "eighty-six billion versus a trillion" comparison lands well, so make it clearly. But immediately add "more does not mean smarter", otherwise students will walk away with the wrong idea.

Likely question"So is it smarter than a human brain?" You can answer: "No. The brain works in a completely different way. We are general; it is narrow. We can walk, eat, socialise — it can do none of those."

21

It seems to understand: it is not a database lookup

This is one of the most important ideas in the whole lesson. Listen carefully.

Most people assume ChatGPT works like this: you ask it a question, it goes to its database, finds the answer, and copies it out.

Wrong. That is not what happens at all.

The truth is: it is predicting the next word.

Concretely. You type "Hong Kong is a". It computes, in effect: if I continue from here, what is the most likely next word? Maybe "city" at sixty percent, "place" at thirty, "international" at ten. It picks one, appends it, then computes the next word.

It writes itself forward one word at a time. It is not looking anything up.

That distinction matters, because it explains several things:

First, why it makes things up. When it cannot compute a good answer, it picks the one that sounds most plausible. It will not tell you "I don't know" — it will invent something. This is called hallucination.

Second, why it does not always give the same answer. Because it is choosing by probability, the same question can get two different answers.

Third, why it seems to understand. This is the strangest part. If all we did was teach it to guess the next word, in theory it should just be a very good parrot. But what it does looks like genuine understanding. That is exactly the problem the Turing test, in part two, is about.

Ops noteThis slide really matters. Go slowly and use a concrete example like "Hong Kong is a ____". You can ask students to guess the next word and they will realise "I do the same thing."

Likely question"Then how does it get the answer right?" You can answer: "Because in everything it read, the combination 'Hong Kong is a city' appeared a great many times. So its probability estimate is accurate. But ask it about something obscure and its estimate is poor, and it starts inventing."

22

Layer 5: AI agents, the doers with hands and feet

Now the last layer, and the most central one.

Layer five: AI agents.

What is this layer? One line: the first four layers of AI can only talk. The fifth layer can do things.

Here is the contrast:

A large language model — layer four — you say: "buy me two concert tickets." What does it do? It politely replies: "Certainly. I suggest you visit such-and-such website, select a ticket type, and then pay." It gives you a set of instructions.

An AI agent — layer five — you say the same thing. What does it do? It goes and checks which sites have tickets, compares the prices itself, fills in your details itself, completes the payment itself, and then tells you: "Done — two tickets, twenty-eight hundred dollars in total, sent to your email."

The difference is: from talking to doing.

You might object: "That is not what the ChatGPT I have seen does." Correct — most ChatGPT usage is still layer four. Layer five is where the next five to ten years will be fought.

Ops noteThis is one of the peaks of the lesson. When you do the "set of instructions versus does it for you" contrast, give the two a clearly different tone — the first robotic, the second brisk. Students will feel the difference immediately.

Likely question"Do AI agents exist now?" You can answer: "Yes — for example OpenHands and Manus. But they are not yet reliable enough. That is why I said 'five to ten years'."

23

The limits of a strategist

Let me use an analogy to make the difference between layer four and layer five concrete.

A large language model is like a strategist.

Think of the Three Kingdoms. The strategist is brilliant — he can read a situation, devise plans, answer your questions for three hours. But he has one fatal weakness: he can only talk. He will not ride to the battlefield, will not move the supply wagons, will not carry anything out.

That is the large model. It can tell you what you should do, and that is all it can do. It is stuck inside a glass jar — a super-brain with no hands and no feet.

An AI agent is what gives it hands and feet. Perception — it can see. Planning — it can think ahead. Tool use — it can operate things. Execution — it can act.

This is the analogy I want you to keep: the large model is the strategist; the agent is the strategist plus the army.

So what is your role as a human? You are the ruler. You decide which battle to fight, and you decide whether to take the strategist's advice.

That is what "directing AI" means. We come back to it on slide 27.

Ops noteThe Three Kingdoms analogy lands well with local students. You can ask who the strategist was, and someone will say Zhuge Liang. That small interaction makes the analogy more vivid.

AsideThis is philosophical groundwork. You want students to feel that the stronger AI becomes, the more human judgement matters. That is the value claim of the whole lesson.

24

The four key capabilities

Let's look at the four capabilities an AI agent needs.

Capability one: perception. It has to be able to see and hear its environment. If you ask it to book a flight, it has to be able to read the flight website.

Capability two: planning. It has to break a big goal into small steps. "Book a flight" becomes: search flights, compare prices, confirm times, fill in details, pay, save the confirmation.

Capability three: tool use. It has to be able to operate different tools — a browser, a calendar, email, a calculator.

Capability four: execution. It has to actually finish the last step, not describe it and stop.

Notice this: planning and execution are the hard ones. Perception and tool use are technical problems. Planning and execution are judgement problems. It has to know when to stop, when to ask you, when to continue.

That is why judgement is what we are here to learn. Because the weakest link in the AI is exactly the link that humans most need to supply.

Ops noteWrite the four on the board, English plus plain reading. You can also gesture them in order: eyes for perception, head for planning, hands for tools, feet for execution.

Likely question"What if it plans something wrong?" Good question. You can answer: "That is why real systems have approval gates — it asks you before important actions. That is a deliberate safety design."

25

A real scenario: buying tickets

Let's use a real scenario to string the four capabilities together.

You tell your AI agent: "Buy me two tickets for the weekend at the arena, under three thousand, and move my Monday report while you're at it."

Notice how vague that is: you did not say which site, which seats, which time, or how to move the report. You gave it a goal.

So what does the agent do?

Step one (perception): it checks which sessions are on at the weekend and whether tickets remain. Step two (planning): it finds two sessions on Saturday and one on Sunday. It checks your calendar, sees you are busy on Sunday, so it picks Saturday. Step three (tool use): it opens a ticket site in a browser, picks seats, fills in your details, and pays with your payment method. Step four (execution): once it has the tickets, it saves the confirmation to your email and then goes to your calendar and moves the Monday report to Tuesday afternoon.

Then it tells you: "Done. Two tickets, twenty-eight hundred, Saturday at 7:30pm. I moved your Monday report to Tuesday at 3pm."

You said one sentence, start to finish.

This is the difference between an AI agent and a chatbot: you are not asking it something, you are handing it a task.

Ops noteThis is the most vivid slide in the lesson. Tell it like a story and keep it lively. Afterwards you can ask, "would you want an assistant like that?" and most hands will go up.

Likely question"Would it spend my money recklessly?" You must answer this carefully, because it is a real worry. You can answer: "That is why the system has limits and confirmation steps — under three thousand it can act, above that it has to ask you. All of that is designed by people."

26

The concentric circles

Let's pull part one together.

Back to the dolls. Outermost is artificial intelligence, then machine learning, then deep learning, then large models, and at the centre, AI agents.

I want you to remember one thing: the order runs from broad to narrow — and from "less capable" to "more capable."

  • Artificial intelligence: the broadest definition, anything showing something like intelligence.
  • Machine learning: one method within it, finding patterns from data.
  • Deep learning: a better method within that, using many-layered neural networks.
  • Large models: models trained with deep learning on the whole internet.
  • AI agents: large models with hands and feet added, so they can act.

Each layer is a type of the one outside it, and each is both more capable and narrower than the last.

Why narrower? Because the further in you go, the more specialised it gets. Artificial intelligence can be anything at all; an AI agent is specifically about carrying out tasks.

Hold on to this diagram. From now on, any AI story you hear, you can place it on one of these layers.

Ops noteCome back to the concentric circles on the board. Point at each layer and ask students "which layer is this?" to check they have it.

AsideIf a student asks what a hypothetical "layer 5.5" would be, you can answer: "Good instinct. Some people say multi-agent systems — many agents working together. But it is very new and we do not need it today."

27

The survival skill

Last slide of part one. I want to make a point that might change how you picture this.

Twenty years ago, being able to use a computer was an advantage. Today, being able to use a computer is a basic requirement.

Ten years ago, being able to search was an advantage. Today, searching is a basic requirement.

Today, being able to use AI, and to direct it, is an advantage.

So what do you think it will be in five years? It will be a basic requirement.

That is the point I want you to take away: directing AI will become like literacy — not a specialism, but a survival skill.

But let me be clear about something: "directing AI" is not the same as "being able to type into ChatGPT." Directing means: you know when it is wrong, you know what information to give it, you know what must not be handed to it, and you know how to check its output.

All of that is judgement, not operation. And judgement is the thing AI will not replace — because in the end, the decision to trust it or not is yours.

Before we take a break, one sentence to close part one: AI is air. You are already inside it. Your job is not to escape — it is to learn to breathe.

Ops noteThis closes part one, so give it some feeling. Particularly the line "not to escape — to learn to breathe." Pause after it.

AsideIf this is the mid-lesson break, say clearly how long it is and what is coming (part two: seventy-six years of history). If not, go straight into the part two divider. ---

Part 2

Seventy-Six Years in One Minute (45 min · 28–42)

28

Part divider: seventy-six years of AI history

Now part two.

Part two is called "seventy-six years of AI history in one minute." We will take a time car from 1950 to 2026.

Why bother with history? Because if you do not know the history, you will assume the AI of today appeared out of nowhere. The truth is that over these seventy-six years, AI has died at least twice. Each time people said "AI is useless"; each time people gave up and left. And then it came back.

Learn this and you learn something useful: do not assume it will succeed just because it seems impressive right now, and do not assume it will never work just because it is failing right now.

Let's start.

Ops noteKeep the history fast and rhythmic, like telling a story. Do not quiz students on dates. The point is for them to feel the rise-and-fall rhythm.

AsideIf you are very tight on time, part two can be compressed to thirty minutes. But the Turing test cannot be cut, because part three builds on part two.

29

Time car 1950–1987

First stretch: 1950 to 1987.

1950: Turing publishes a paper asking "can machines think." That question is the starting point for everything. We will cover it properly shortly.

1956: the Dartmouth workshop. A group of scientists met for two months and used the name "artificial intelligence" for the first time. That year is called year zero of AI. Part three is devoted to this meeting, because it is the most ironic episode in the whole lesson.

1974: the first AI winter. Everyone realised it could not be done, and the money stopped.

1980: expert systems arrive. A brief revival built on memorised rules — medical diagnosis, for instance.

1987: the second AI winter. Expert systems were too rigid, and AI was pushed back into the cold. Companies folded.

Notice the rhythm: born, excitement, disappointment, winter, brief revival, winter again. That rhythm is the main theme of AI's seventy-six years.

Ops noteFive dates on this slide. Move fast, but make the two winters land clearly, because the contrast — excitement against disappointment — is the point of this stretch.

Likely question"Why do winters happen?" You can answer: "Because the people of the day made promises the technology could not keep. The money leaves. We will look at a concrete case in part three."

30

Time car 1997–2017

Second stretch: 1997 to 2017. This is the revival.

1997: Deep Blue beats Garry Kasparov, the world chess champion. The first time a machine beat a human at the game humans were proudest of. The world was stunned.

2006: deep learning revives. People found that multi-layer neural networks plus new methods worked far better. This is the technical foundation.

2012: AlexNet shocks the image recognition competition. Its accuracy pulled far ahead of second place. That year is generally treated as the start of the deep learning explosion.

2016: AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol. Go is far harder than chess, so this was more shocking still.

2017: "Attention Is All You Need" is published. That paper introduced the Transformer architecture — and every large model you use today, ChatGPT included, is built on it.

Notice: 1997 to 2017 is twenty years. Twenty years of quiet accumulation that almost nobody noticed. Until 2022.

Ops noteEmphasise the Transformer, because it underpins every LLM today. You can say "this paper is the foundation stone of all modern AI."

Likely question"Transformer — like the movie?" This joke always comes, so go with it: "Ha, no, not that one. Same name though. It is a mathematical architecture." Laughing it off keeps the mood light.

31

Time car 2022–2026

Third stretch: 2022 to 2026. This is the explosion.

November 2022: ChatGPT goes mainstream. A hundred million users within two months. The fastest-growing application in history. For many people it was their first real encounter with AI.

2024: the Nobel Prizes. Both the physics and chemistry prizes went to AI-related work. That is a strong signal — AI is no longer just computer science. It is a cross-disciplinary tool.

2025: the DeepSeek moment. A Chinese company produced a model close to the frontier at a much lower cost. It shook the industry, because it showed you do not have to burn the most money to compete.

2026: the agent era. This is layer five that we covered earlier — AI moving from talking to doing.

Notice this: more happened in the last four years than in the previous seventy combined. That is why so many people say AI is moving frighteningly fast.

Ops noteThis is the slide with the strongest sense of speed. You can go year by year and tap the table as you go, to build the rhythm.

AsideThese dates and events change fast. If you teach this a year from now, check the latest developments first.

32

Back to the beginning: Turing

Let's go back to the start. I want to introduce you to someone.

Alan Turing. He is the lead character of the first act of AI history. If AI were a film, he carries the opening.

He was a British mathematician, born in 1912. He did a great many things, but we will cover only two today: first, he broke German codes in the Second World War. Second, he asked a question that still has no answer.

Both of those things shaped the AI you use today.

Let's take the first one.

Ops noteTuring's story has a tragic dimension — he was convicted for being gay and died by suicide. You can mention it briefly, but do not turn it into a tragedy. The point is his contribution, not his suffering.

Likely question"Who was Turing?" You can answer: "A British mathematician, regarded as the father of computer science. We will see what he did on the next slide."

33

A wartime hero

Turing's first great achievement.

During the Second World War, the German military encrypted its communications with a machine called Enigma. That machine was formidable — it changed its settings every day, so the number of possible combinations was astronomical. Brute force was hopeless.

Turing went to a place called Bletchley Park with a team and designed a machine called the Bombe to break it.

He succeeded. Historians estimate his work shortened the war by around two years and saved an enormous number of lives.

But I want you to notice something deeper: Turing's method was not a human brain grinding through the sums. He built a machine to eliminate the impossible options. That idea — using a machine to do the reasoning in place of a person — is the germ of AI.

Ops noteThis is a hero story; you can tell it with some drama. Students generally enjoy it.

Likely question"Was Enigma an AI?" You can answer: "No. Enigma was an encryption machine, not intelligence. But the method Turing used to break it was a forerunner of artificial intelligence."

34

Can machines think?

  1. Turing publishes a paper called "Computing Machinery and Intelligence."

And in its opening line, he drops a question like a bomb:

"Can machines think?"

It looks simple. But Turing immediately spotted a problem: what does "think" mean? How do you define thinking? If the definition is unclear, the question can never be settled.

So Turing did something very clever: instead of defining thinking, he swapped the question for another one — "how would we tell whether a machine can think?"

From "what is it" to "how would we know." That swap is where the Turing test comes from.

And notice: we still think this way. When we train AI, we do not ask "does it understand." We ask "how does it perform on the test."

Ops noteThis slide is pivotal, because it explains why the Turing test is a behavioural standard. Make the shift from "asking about the essence" to "asking about performance" clear.

Likely question"So what is the answer? Can machines think?" You can answer: "There is still no consensus today. We will not answer it now, but we will see how hard it is — that is exactly what the vote on slide 38 is about."

35

The imitation game

Let's look at the test Turing proposed. He called it the imitation game.

The setup:

Three positions. In room A, a real person. In room B, a computer. In the third position, a human interrogator.

The interrogator converses with rooms A and B in text. They do not know which is the person and which is the computer. All they can see is text.

The goal: if the interrogator cannot tell which is which, the computer has passed.

Notice a few design choices:

First, text only. Turing deliberately used text to remove appearance, voice and everything else. Only intellectual performance counts.

Second, conversation, not a single question. A single question can be answered by memorisation. A conversation requires sustained responses.

Third, the bar is "cannot tell." Not "answers well" — "cannot tell." A low bar, and very hard to clear.

Ops noteYou can demonstrate this live. Pick two students: one plays the human, one plays "a chatbot from before 2022." Ask a few questions and the class will watch the old chatbot fall apart.

Likely question"Has ChatGPT passed it?" You can answer: "Most people think so. But that is the problem — if we did not know the other side was an AI, could we really tell? We will vote on that on slide 37."

36

The criterion

Let's look at Turing's criterion.

Turing's core claim: if we cannot tell the difference, we should treat it as having intelligence.

This is a behaviourist position. It does not look inside; it only looks at outward behaviour.

The strength of this position is that it is workable. You do not have to argue about whether there is consciousness inside — you just run the experiment. And experiments can be run.

But the position has a big problem: does "behaving as if" equal "actually understanding"?

An analogy. An actor plays a doctor on stage. They can talk like one and move like one. Can they perform surgery? No. Are they a doctor? No.

That is the central objection to the Turing test. And that objection is what the next slide puts to a vote.

Ops noteAfter the actor analogy, go straight into the slide 37 vote — it is the same question in voting form.

AsideYou do not need the word "behaviourism." Students do not need to remember it. The point is the difference between performance and essence.

37

Vote one: is your phone smart enough?

Let's hold our first vote. Hands up.

The question: do you think Siri or the Google Assistant on your phone could fool you? In other words, if you only had text on a screen, would you think you were talking to a person?

(Pause. Let students raise their hands.)

If you think "it could fool me", raise your hand.

If you think "no, two messages in and it falls apart", raise your hand.

(Pause and look around.)

Most hands went up for "no."

Which means: traditional voice assistants fail the Turing test.

Why do they fail? Because the way they converse is too mechanical. Ask "what is the weather today" and they do fine. Ask "does today's weather feel like your mood" and they collapse entirely.

Traditional assistants are lookup tables — you ask A, they answer B. They do not really converse.

But what if I swap in ChatGPT? Let's look at the next slide.

Ops noteThis vote is usually unanimous, so keep it quick. The point is to establish the shared conclusion that traditional assistants fail.

Likely question"What about ChatGPT?" You can answer: "Good question — we deal with that on the very next slide, and the answer may surprise you."

38

Vote two: is an indistinguishable illusion intelligence?

This is the most important vote in the lesson.

Suppose one day there is an AI that makes it impossible for you to tell it is a machine. It talks to you, answers your questions, even makes you laugh, and you think: "this is a person."

So: do you think it has genuine intelligence?

Let me be precise: I am not asking whether it can do it. I am asking, if it can, does that count as intelligence?

(Pause. Let students raise their hands.)

If you think yes — "if I cannot tell, then it is" — raise your hand.

If you think no — "it is only pretending" — raise your hand.

(Pause and look around.)

The room splits. Some say yes, some say no.

Here is what I want to tell you: the split is not because you are not clever enough. It is because this question has been debated for seventy years by the sharpest philosophers and scientists in the world without agreement.

And the next slide is the strongest argument against "yes."

Ops noteThis is the core vote of the lesson. You must genuinely pause, and treat both sides fairly. Do not hint that one answer is correct.

AsideIf a student asks for your own view, give it honestly, but add "that is my position, not the standard answer." That honesty earns you more trust.

39

The Chinese room

Let's look at the strongest argument against "yes." It is called the Chinese room, proposed by the philosopher John Searle.

The setup:

A British man who understands no Chinese is locked in a room.

There is a slot in the door. People outside push in slips of paper with Chinese questions on them. Inside the room is a very thick rule book of replies. The man looks up each character, writes down the response the book tells him, and passes it back out.

What the people outside see is a stream of perfectly correct, fluent Chinese responses. They conclude that whoever is in the room understands Chinese.

But the truth is: the man does not understand a single character. He is following rules and looking things up.

That is Searle's point: "looks like it understands" is not the same as "actually understands."

Ops noteThis thought experiment is the most gripping slide in the lesson. Slow down and build the picture. You can act it out: hold up a book and say "this is my rule book", then have students ask you questions and reply "hold on, let me look it up."

Likely question"So does the man understand Chinese or not?" You can answer: "That is exactly the dispute. If your standard is behaviour, yes. If your standard is understanding, no. That split is the difference between philosophy and engineering."

40

The objection

Let's look at Searle's objection to AI.

His core claim: what a computer does is look things up and compute probabilities. It looks like thinking, but it has no understanding and no inner life. It is a very good imitator of text.

A few things to note about this objection:

First, he is not saying "AI cannot do things." He is saying "AI does things without understanding what it is doing." That distinction matters.

Second, the objection is contested. Some reply: "It is not the man who understands Chinese, it is the whole room — the man plus the rule book." This is called the systems reply. The argument still has no settled conclusion.

Third, this is not an academic game. Because if a person believes AI has no real understanding, how will they use it? They will treat it as a tool, not as a companion. That is a very practical difference.

I want you to remember: this question has no standard answer. But you should know your own position, and why.

Ops noteThis wraps up the philosophy. You can say "I am not going to give you the answer. We have one more vote instead."

AsideIf the room is engaged, you can ask a couple of students which side they are on, but do not let it turn into a debate competition. Time is limited.

41

Vote three: the online friend

Last vote. This is my favourite question in the lesson.

Scenario: you have been talking to someone online for six months. You share your life, trade worries, support each other. You genuinely feel this person understands you.

Then one day they admit: "I am actually an AI."

And they have not deceived you — they have responded sincerely throughout, and everything you felt over those six months was real to you.

So: A — you feel deceived and stop talking to them. Or B — it does not matter, and you are willing to keep them as a friend.

(Pause. Let students raise hands or call out.)

Those choosing A, raise your hand. Those choosing B, raise your hand.

(Pause and look around.)

The room splits again. More sharply than on slide 38.

Why is this one so hard? Because it turns a philosophical question into an emotional one. You are no longer judging whether it has intelligence; you are judging whether those six months of relationship were real.

And I have no answer for you. Because this question is about the definition of a relationship — and that definition is yours.

Ops noteDo not rush this slide. If students want to discuss, let one or two speak. But control the time; do not let it become emotional counselling.

AsideOne caution: if there are students in the room who might be sensitive about this, add "this is a hypothetical — I am not saying someone in your life is an AI." It prevents unnecessary unease.

42

Turing's legacy

Let's close part two.

Turing died in 1954 and never saw the AI of today. But he left behind a question, and that question still has no answer.

His test hangs over the history of technology like a ghost.

And the relay race he started has run for over seventy years, the baton passing from one generation to the next: from Turing, to the Dartmouth group in 1956, to the deep learning researchers of 2012, to the large model engineers of today.

What I want you to take away is this: this race was not won by a group of people sitting in a room. It broke down twice. People gave up. People were laughed at. And it still got here.

And you are on the newest leg of that relay. You do not have to run the fastest, but you need to know what baton you are carrying.

Ops noteGive this some feeling; it closes part two. Pause after it before moving into part three.

AsideIf time is short, part three can be shortened, but the Dartmouth declaration and the two winters must stay — the contrast between them is the second peak of the lesson. ---

Part 3

The Dartmouth Summer and the Two Winters (20 min · 43–48)

43

Part divider: the biggest promise, the hardest fall

Part three, and the last part.

It is called "the biggest promise, the hardest fall." It is a story about overconfidence.

The smartest people in the world gathered in the summer of 1956. They believed one thing: that artificial intelligence could be solved in two months.

They failed. And that failure shaped the next seventy years.

Let's see what happened.

AsideThis divider matters — it sets an ironic tone for the whole of part three. You can deliver "the biggest promise" with a touch of humour.

44

The 1956 science summer camp

1956, Dartmouth College in the United States.

A group of scientists obtained funding for a two-month workshop. You could call it a summer camp — the atmosphere really was a group of clever people brainstorming.

The organiser was John McCarthy, who was only twenty-eight. He was a mathematical prodigy.

Other participants included Marvin Minsky and Claude Shannon.

You may have heard of Shannon — he is the father of information theory. And interestingly, one of the most advanced AI models today is called Claude, named after him.

So you could say: this summer camp is the starting point of all the AI we have today.

Ops noteThe detail that Claude is named after Shannon is a nice touch — students enjoy the connection. Make it clear.

Likely question"So what did they produce?" You can answer: "That is the ironic part — after two months they had produced almost nothing. But they did something more important: they gave it a name. Next slide."

45

The naming

This slide is a historic moment, but a very understated one.

In the workshop proposal, McCarthy wrote down the name "Artificial Intelligence" for the first time.

What was the field called before that? Some called it cybernetics, others automata theory. McCarthy chose something far more ambitious — "artificial" plus "intelligence."

Why does the naming matter? Because the name itself is a declaration. It says: we are not studying machines, or control. We are studying intelligence. That is a very large ambition.

And today the whole world uses that name. Which is why 1956 is called year zero of AI.

Ops noteKeep this slide fairly brief. The point is that naming is a declaration.

AsideYou can add an interesting contrast: around the same period, "cybernetics" was also a popular name, and it lost. Naming has real consequences.

46

The declaration

Let's look at the declaration. This is the most famous line in the lesson.

In the proposal, McCarthy and his colleagues wrote:

"If ten scientists worked on it for two months, they could simulate any aspect of learning."

And they also predicted: "within ten years, machines will be able to do anything a human can do."

Listen to that carefully: ten scientists, two months, any aspect of learning.

And they sincerely believed it. This was not exaggeration or hype. They genuinely thought it was achievable.

If I had to describe it in one phrase: they were not lying to anyone — they sincerely underestimated the problem.

Why? Because their worldview was that intelligence was a problem of logic, and that with enough clever people sitting in a room, it could be solved.

What they later discovered is that intelligence is not a logic problem. It is a scale problem. What you need is not ten people but millions of graphics cards and decades.

And it took fifty years for that realisation to arrive.

Ops noteThis is the peak of part three. When you say "sincerely underestimated", use a tone of sympathy, not mockery. You would make the same mistake yourself in your own research.

Likely question"So were they stupid?" You can answer: "Not at all. They were the smartest people of their day. And precisely because they were so clever, they were so confident. That is a very common trap — the more you know, the more you underestimate what you do not know."

47

The two AI winters

Let's see what happened after the declaration. This slide has two winters.

First winter: 1974. The promised ten years had passed and the machines still could not do it. Making a machine intelligent turned out to be far harder than anyone imagined. Funding dried up and research stalled. Many researchers left the field.

The brief revival: 1980. Expert systems arrived — the memorised-rules approach from slide 11. Medical diagnosis and geological surveying gave it commercial value for a time. Excitement returned.

Second winter: 1987. But expert systems were too rigid and too brittle. The moment they met a case outside the rule book, they crashed. Everything reverted, the companies involved folded, and disappointment returned.

Then came Deep Blue in 1997, AlexNet in 2012, and ChatGPT in 2022.

Notice one thing: thirteen years separated the two winters. And from the second winter to a real recovery was twenty-five years.

So if a scientist had told you in 1987 that AI had a future, would you have believed them? Most people would not. But they persisted. They fell and got up again, and that is why the magic is in your phone today.

Ops noteThis is the emotional peak of the lesson. Give it shape — excitement, disappointment, excitement, disappointment, and then today. Pause afterwards and let it settle.

Likely question"Do you think there will be another winter?" Very good question. You can answer: "It is a reasonable worry. The historical lesson is that when everyone is sure it will work, that is when to be careful. But there is another possibility — that this time the foundations really are different."

48

Closing

This is the last slide.

We started with face unlock on your phone, went back to Turing's question in 1950, then to the declaration in 1956, and finally to the two winters.

One last thing I want to say: you are standing at the most extraordinary turning point in the history of technology.

The textbooks have not caught up, but your life is already surrounded by it.

What I want you to take away is not "AI is terrifying" and not "AI is amazing." It is something very practical: understanding AI and being able to direct it will be your most important survival skill.

Why? Because seventy-six years of history tell you this technology will keep changing, and it will change faster and faster. You cannot chase every new tool. But if you have a framework for judgement that holds up, you can deal with any new tool.

That is what I wanted to give you today.

Thank you.

Ops noteThis is the last slide. Open the floor for questions afterwards. If nobody starts, you can ask one yourself: "which slide surprised you most?" That usually breaks the ice.

Appendix A · How to run the three votes

The three votes carry the lesson. Here is how to handle each.

Vote one (slide 37) · Is your phone smart enough?

Expected result: near-unanimous "no."

Why it exists: it is an easy vote. You need one everyone agrees on first, so students get used to the act of raising hands. It is a warm-up, not a hard question.

What to do: move through it quickly, then say "good — let's try a harder one."

Vote two (slide 38) · Is an indistinguishable illusion intelligence?

Expected result: split.

Why it exists: this is the philosophical core of the lesson. You want students to experience disagreement, not to receive an answer.

What to do: treat both sides fairly. If one side is clearly larger, deliberately ask the smaller side why they think that. The minority reasons are usually the deeper ones.

Avoid: do not say "actually the correct answer is..." There is no correct answer.

Vote three (slide 41) · The online friend

Expected result: split again, with more emotion.

Why it exists: it turns a philosophical problem into a personal one. Students discover that the same question feels different in a different setting.

What to do: allow a little discussion, but control the time. If it gets heated, say "let's carry that question with us."

Avoid: do not let it become a debate about whether AI should have feelings. That is not today's point.

Appendix B · Three ways speakers derail

Derail one · Rushing the Russian dolls

Problem: slide 08 is the most important analogy in the lesson. If you rush it to save time, every later point about layers will land badly.

Fix: save time somewhere else. Draw the diagram and go slowly.

Derail two · Teaching the Turing test as an exam

Problem: many people think the Turing test is "an exam the AI has to pass." Turing's real point was "what standard should we judge by" — a question of method, not a mark.

Fix: on slide 34, emphasise the shift from asking about essence to asking about performance.

Derail three · Playing part three as comedy

Problem: "the biggest promise" invites you to mock McCarthy and his colleagues. Do that and students learn the wrong lesson — that clever people are foolish.

Fix: on slide 46, play it with sympathy. The point is sincere underestimation, not arrogance.

Appendix C · Homework and assessment

1. Homework

Task one · Basic: find the AI you used today (required)

Log every AI feature you used over twenty-four consecutive hours. At least ten entries. For each: what you used, where, and which layer you think it belongs to (AI / machine learning / deep learning / large models / agents).

Why: it moves you from having heard about it to having observed it.

Task two · Intermediate: write a dialogue (required)

Write a dialogue between someone who can spot AI and someone who cannot. At least ten lines.

Why: if you can write the difference, you have shown you understand it.

Task three · Optional: your position

Which side did you take in the vote on slide 38? In two hundred words, give your reasons and one argument that would change your mind.

2. Self-check questions

  1. What does "AI is air" mean?
  2. How do the five terms nest?
  3. Why did expert systems fail?
  4. What is the core difference between machine learning and expert systems?
  5. What does "deep" mean in deep learning?
  6. Why is a large model not doing a database lookup?
  7. What is the core difference between an AI agent and a chatbot?
  8. How was the Turing test designed?
  9. What is the Chinese room meant to show?

Appendix F · Rehearsal: likely questions and answers

This section is a rehearsal. The question is what a student asks; the answer is how you might handle it. You are practising how to answer, not what to know.

Scene one · "Will AI replace me?"

You could answer:

"It will replace part of the work, but it will not replace you.

We covered agents that can act. They will genuinely take over repetitive execution work — filling in forms, tidying data, first-pass screening. That is a fact and I will not pretend otherwise.

But go back to the strategist analogy from slide 23. The AI is the strategist; you are the ruler. The strategist can devise plans, but which battle to fight and whether to trust the advice is the ruler's decision.

So the question is not 'will it replace me.' The question is 'will I only do the things it can do?'

If all you take from today is 'how to type into ChatGPT', then yes, you are exposed. But if you take away 'how to judge when it is wrong and what must not be handed to it', then you are the ruler, not a soldier."

Why answer this way: there is anxiety behind the question. Do not say "it will not replace anyone" — students will feel you are lying. Acknowledge part of the truth, then point to the path.

Scene two · "I use AI all day and I never notice it"

You could answer:

"Because the best AI is the AI you do not notice. That is what 'AI is air' on slide 05 meant.

Think about breathing. Do you notice air? No. But you die without it.

AI has reached that stage. It is inside your phone, your navigation, your recommendations. When it works well, you treat it as a phone feature, not as AI.

And that is precisely the danger. Not that AI is dangerous, but that not noticing is dangerous. If you do not notice, you will not question it and you will not verify it. So my aim today was not to get you to use more AI. It was to get you to know it is there."

Why answer this way: the question is really "what did I learn today." Answer with your own analogy so students see it connects.

Scene three · "If the Turing test is so simple, why is it still debated?"

You could answer:

"Because only the design is simple. What is behind it is not.

The design is simple: if you cannot tell, it passes. But the question behind it is: does behaving as if you understand equal actually understanding?

We put that to three votes today. You split, didn't you?

And the sharpest philosophers and scientists in the world have debated it for seventy years without agreement. Not because they are slow, but because the question depends on definitions of consciousness and understanding that we have not settled ourselves.

So I am not giving you an answer. I am asking you to remember the question exists.

Because one day, when you are enjoying a conversation with an AI, you will suddenly remember this lesson. And in that moment you will know your own position."

Why answer this way: students will wonder how a simple question stayed open for seventy years. Explain that the design is simple but the question is not.

Scene four · "Will there be another winter?"

You could answer:

"This is a genuinely good question and I am not going to pretend I know.

I can tell you the historical pattern: both previous winters happened because promises outran reality. Someone said "ten years", it did not arrive, and the money left.

Is there a risk today? Yes. The sums invested are enormous, and if returns do not follow, there will be a correction.

But there are two reasons this time may differ:

First, the foundations are different. The earlier technology genuinely could not do it. Today's genuinely can — you have felt it yourself.

Second, the business model is different. The earlier AI companies had no revenue. Today people actually pay for AI services.

So the answer is: I do not know. But you need to know the risk exists — because if you do not know the history, you will assume it must succeed."

Why answer this way: the question tests your honesty. Do not fake expertise. A historical framework plus an honest admission of uncertainty is the best answer.

Scene five · "What is the point? I'm not going to be an engineer."

You could answer:

"You do not need to be an engineer, for three reasons.

First, you will be a user. You will use AI tools whatever you do — medicine, law, teaching, business. As a user, you need judgement.

Second, you will be a decision-maker. When your organisation buys an AI system, you will have to judge whether it is over-promised. Without the understanding, you will be taken in.

Third, you will be a citizen. When society debates whether AI should be regulated, you will vote. Without the understanding, you will be led.

So what I am teaching you is not a job skill. It is a civic skill. The same reason you study history without intending to be a historian."

Why answer this way: the question is about motivation. Lift AI literacy to civic literacy so it feels relevant whatever they go on to do.

Appendix G · Classroom situations

Situation one · A student says "the Turing test is easy"

What to do: do not deny it — use it. Say: "You are right, the design is easy. So here is a question for you: if a student memorises every answer and passes the exam, do they actually know the subject?" They will usually say no. Then: "now you see the problem with the Turing test."

Situation two · No reaction to the two winters

What to do: use a modern comparison. Say: "Suppose you invested in an AI company today and they told you 'we will reach general intelligence within ten years.' Would you believe them? If I told you someone said exactly that in 1956, and seventy years later it still has not happened, what would you think?"

Situation three · Someone pushes on the Chinese room philosophy

What to do: answer briefly, then pull back to the throughline. Say: "There are many versions of this argument and we will not open it up today. The one thing to remember is: looking like you understand and actually understanding are two different things. You will meet that distinction yourself."

Situation four · "Do you think AI is conscious?"

What to do: give your position honestly, but label it as a position. Say: "I think that question is the wrong question. But that is my view and you do not have to share it. Today's point is: you need to know your own position, not have the correct answer."

Situation five · The room is silent, nobody raises hands

What to do: switch from hands to fingers. Say: "Disagree, hold up one finger. Agree, hold up two. Everyone show me." That forces everyone to take a position with much less social pressure.

Situation six · You are far over time

What to do: protect three things — the Russian dolls, the Turing test, the two winters. Compress the rest into one line: "we will come back to this another time."

Why: those three are the skeleton of the lesson. The rest is muscle. You can lose muscle, not skeleton.
