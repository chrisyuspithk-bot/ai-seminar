# AI-Era Detective Class — English Script

> Companion deck: `deepfake-deck.html` (33 slides)
> Language: English (translated from the Cantonese script)
> How to use: one section per slide. The first block in each section is the script you read aloud. The "Ops note", "Aside" and "Likely question" blocks are speaker reminders and are not read out.
> Speaker note: keep sentences short, one idea per slide. The first time a technical term appears, give the English term and then explain it in plain words.

---

## How to use this script (read first)

1. This script maps page by page to the deck. Each page holds roughly two to three minutes of material. If you are short on time, read only the bolded sentences in each section.
2. "You" means the whole class. If you are teaching one to one, switch everything to the singular.
3. **Update the crime statistics before you teach.** They change every year and the enforcement agencies publish new figures.
4. The four-step method — stop, look, check, find — is the spine of the lesson. Write it on the board at slide 15 and point at it on every subsequent slide.
5. The lesson has one throughline: **the naked eye is unreliable; a process is reliable.** The aim is not to make students suspicious of everything, but to teach them to believe with discipline.

# Session 1 · True or false, the phenomenon (30 min · 01–11)

## Slide 01 · Cover: the AI-era detective class

Hello everyone, and welcome to the AI-era detective class.

Let me start with a number. **In the first seven months of 2026, Hong Kong recorded two thousand and twenty deepfake-related crimes, up one point seven times on the same period last year. A single voice-cloning fraud involved seventeen million Hong Kong dollars.**

Why open with a number? Because I do not want you to think this is a future problem or a tech-news problem. **It is happening now, and it is happening around us.**

But let me be clear about one thing: **I am not here to teach you to trust nothing.**

If you leave this class unable to trust anything, I have failed. Because a society that believes nothing is exactly as dangerous as a society that believes everything.

**What I want to teach you is believing with discipline.**

What does with discipline mean? It means relying not on feeling but on a process. You will learn a four-step method called stop, look, check, find. It will not turn you into a forensic expert, but it will give you the ability to slow down at the moment you most need to.

There are four sessions today. **Session one:** no theory to begin with. We start with a quiz to see how easily your eyes and your brain can be fooled, then look at real cases. **Session two:** the technical principles — generative adversarial networks and four kinds of deepfake — and where the flaws usually hide. **After a short break, session three:** law and ethics — where the law reaches, use the law; where it does not, use judgement. **Session four:** summary and action.

**Today's goal: by the end of the class you will not be a suspicious person. You will be a person who knows how to verify.**

> **Ops note** Prepare in advance: a face image for the warm-up, voice samples (three sets), and classroom exercise material (three to four sets, ranging from easy to hard). Test the playback equipment and audio. Confirm the network can reach the fact-checking platforms.

## Slide 02 · Course map: today we train the eye for verification

Here is the map. Three stages.

**First, see the phenomenon.** From Hong Kong to the mainland, AI face-swap and voice-cloning fraud is rising fast, in both amounts and case counts. In this part I want you to **feel** it, not just know it.

**Second, understand the principles.** Meet generative adversarial networks and the four kinds of deepfake. Why the principles? Because **if you do not know how the technology works, you do not know where the flaws hide.** You will judge on a vague sense that something is off, and feeling is the least reliable guide.

**Third, practise judgement.** Use the four-step method, plus legal awareness and three ethical principles, to make responsible choices.

**And I want to stress the aim: not that you believe nothing, but that you add one more step of verification before you believe.**

That distinction matters. Disbelief is an endpoint — if you do not believe, you no longer have to think. Verification is a process — you have to do something. What I want you to take away is a process, not a conclusion.

> **Teaching note** An analogy works well here: crossing the road, you do not refuse to cross because there might be a car. You look both ways first. That look is verification. It makes believing with discipline click immediately.

## Slide 03 · Session 1 divider

Session one. I want to do one thing first: **no theory. A quiz.**

Why a quiz before any theory? Because if I open by telling you deepfakes are sophisticated, you will nod, but you will not feel it. I want you to **fail once, yourself.** When you look at a synthesised face, decide it must be real, and then I tell you it is fake — at that moment, you genuinely understand that this concerns you.

So we have three rounds. Round one, a face image. Round two, a synthesised voice. Round three, the answers.

**Let me say now: you may well get this wrong. Most people do.** That is not a fault in you. It is how the human brain is built.

Ready?

> **Ops note** This warm-up is the most important interactive design in the whole class. **Have students write down their reasoning before you reveal the answers.** If you reveal first, the activity is worthless.

## Slide 04 · Opening activity: is this face real?

Round one. Look at this face. **I need you to look at it and write down: real or fake, and what you are basing that on.**

(Let them write, about a minute.)

I hear some of you saying the eyes look unnatural, the skin is too smooth, the hairline is odd. Those are good observations.

**Round two.** I will play a clip. It is a distress call from a grandson to his grandfather.

(Play it.)

**I want you to answer one question: if you were that grandfather, would you believe it?**

(Pause and collect answers.)

Some say yes, some say they would ask more questions first. That reaction is normal.

**Round three: the answers.**

(Reveal.)

**And now a research finding: human accuracy at detecting deepfakes is often only slightly better than chance.**

If you flipped a coin you would be fifty percent right. Judging by eye, you might be fifty-five or sixty. **That is not because you are foolish. It is because when your brain evolved, deepfakes did not exist.**

**That is the first thing I want you to remember today: your eyes and ears are not reliable identification tools.**

> **Teaching note** The key here is collecting the reasoning. Write the students' reasons on the board. After revealing the answers, go back through them one by one: is this reason reliable? That process teaches them which clues are reliable and which are just instinct.

## Slide 05 · Why are we so easily fooled?

Why are we so easily fooled? Because deepfakes attack not your knowledge but your instincts.

**First, your brain's shortcuts.**

Your face recognition is fast and intuitive — you know at a glance whether someone is your mother. But that ability **is not precise.** It is a good-enough mechanism, because during evolution you only needed to tell whether someone was from your tribe. You did not need to detect a synthesised image.

**Deepfakes are built precisely to exploit that fast-judgement weakness.** They do not need to fool you completely. They only need you to feel, at first glance, that nothing is wrong.

**Second, emotional hijacking.**

You get a call saying your grandchild is in trouble and needs money urgently. Your emotions are engaged instantly — panic, fear, an urge to act. **And in that state your ability to verify shuts down.**

That is not a weakness. It is your humanity. And it is exactly what fraudsters exploit.

**So the conclusion is: identification cannot rely on the naked eye. It must rely on a process.**

Because the eye is fast, and a process is deliberately slow. You need a deliberately designed sequence that slows you down, to counter these instinctive reactions.

**That is the design principle behind stop, look, check, find in session two.** It is not a set of identification techniques. It is a delay mechanism.

> **Likely question** "If I am normally careful, won't I be safe?" Careful is good, but careful is not an action you can perform. The four steps are four actions you can perform. That distinction matters.

## Slide 06 · What is a deepfake?

Now the definition. Deepfake is a portmanteau: **deep learning plus fake.** The name itself says where it comes from — it is fake material generated by AI.

But let me make the distinction clear with a comparison: **deepfakes and traditional forgery are two different things.**

**Traditional forgery alters.** Photo editing, cutting and splicing, hiring a voice actor. You take a photo and remove a person with Photoshop. You take a video and cut a section out. You hire someone to record a fake voice. The features are that **there are more traces and the cost is higher**, because you need the original material and you must work through each detail.

**Deepfakes generate.** You train a model on large amounts of data and it automatically generates near-convincing faces, voices and movements. The feature here is that **the cost keeps falling.**

**And the critical difference is this: a deepfake does not edit existing footage. It conjures a scene that never happened.**

Why does that matter? Because it changes your verification strategy.

Faced with an altered photo, you can find the original and compare. But faced with something generated, **there is no original** — because the event never happened. You cannot find the truth to compare against. You can only verify from other angles.

**So generation is harder to check than alteration.** That is why we need a new approach, rather than looking for the original.

> **Aside** Demonstrate this live: edit a photo with a phone app and place it beside an AI-generated face. Students will see that the edited photo has traces, like unnatural edges, while the generated face has no original to compare with. That makes the distinction concrete.

## Slide 07 · This is not future tense, it is present continuous

Let's look at the numbers.

I mentioned them at the start; here is more detail. **In the first seven months of 2026, Hong Kong recorded two thousand and twenty deepfake-related crimes.** That is an absolute number. **The year-on-year rise is one point seven times.** That is a trend. **And a single voice-cloning fraud involved seventeen million Hong Kong dollars.** That is a scale.

**And the most important judgement: this has moved from technical demonstration to a tool of organised crime.**

That sentence deserves a pause. What is a technical demonstration? Look what I can do. What is organised crime? Someone is using this technology to extract money, with division of labour and planning.

**When a technology moves from demonstration to tool, its nature changes.** It is no longer a question of whether it is possible, but of when it reaches you.

But I do not want you alarmed. Look at what sits behind those numbers: **most victims are not there because they lack technical knowledge. They are there because their emotions were engaged.**

Look at the numbers again. Why would someone hand over seventeen million to a phone call? Because they believed the voice. And they believed the voice because it sounded like someone they knew.

**That is not ignorance. That is trust being exploited.** And trust is what we are here to protect.

> **Teaching note** Show the numbers as a simple bar chart, 2025 against 2026. The visual gives one point seven times some weight. **And check the figures before every delivery**, because agencies publish new statistics annually.

## Slide 08 · Case one: a familiar voice can be fake

First case. This one is about voice.

**Fraudsters obtain a few seconds of recording and can synthesise a near-identical voice.** A few seconds. Think about how many videos of you exist on social media. How many voice messages have you sent?

Then they use that voice to impersonate a child or grandchild in distress. **A single case involved roughly seventeen million Hong Kong dollars.**

I especially want you to notice why that number is so large. Because when a person believes a relative is in trouble on the other end of the line, they will use the fastest method and the most money available. **The fraudsters are not designing a scheme to make you fall for something. They are designing a scheme to make you afraid to verify.**

Because you think: if I verify, I lose time, and my child will be hurt.

**So what do you do? Here is a concrete action: hang up first, then call the person back on a number you already knew.**

Notice two parts to that. **Part one is hang up first.** That matters, because as long as you stay on the call, your emotions keep being manipulated. **Part two is the number you already knew.** Do not call back the number they called from, because that number may also be fake. Use the number already in your phone, or one you know by memory.

**That sentence — hang up first, then call back on a number you already knew — is the most practical line today. If you remember only one thing, remember that.**

> **Teaching note** Do a role play here: you play the fraudster, a student plays the parent. Demonstrate the rhythm of urgency and emotional pressure, then demonstrate the correct action — hanging up. It turns hang up first from advice into an experience.

## Slide 09 · Case two: an organised AI fraud ring

Second case. In January 2026, Hong Kong police broke up a gang using AI to defraud people.

**Three numbers: thirty-one people arrested, up to thirty-four million Hong Kong dollars involved, and the members included students.**

That last point, let me pause on it. **The members included students.**

That is the most important part of this case, because it represents one thing: **taking part in deepfake fraud is a criminal offence, and young people can stumble into it.**

Why do I stress this? Because I worry about a mindset: I only handled the technical side, I only helped make the video, I did not know what they would use it for. That mindset is dangerous.

**Legally, if you took part, you took part.** Being responsible for only one part does not make you innocent. And inside a gang like that, only responsible for one part is usually a self-comfort. You knew what those videos were for.

**So let me say one thing to the young people here: if someone asks you to do something that is technically possible but feels wrong, that feeling is accurate.**

Do not take part because it is easy money or because you are only doing a small piece. Because the moment you take part, you move from being someone who knows technology to being a criminal. That change takes a second. Its effect lasts a lifetime.

> **Teaching note** Be careful with the tone here. **Do not use the tone of a judge. Use the tone of a warning.** You may be facing young people who have not yet decided, and you want them to listen to you rather than defend against you.

## Slide 10 · Mainland case one: the perfect match on screen

Two cases from the mainland.

The first is romance fraud. A fraudster used AI face swapping to impersonate a dating partner on video, built trust, then asked for a transfer. About a hundred and thirty thousand was lost, and the sentence was three years, suspended for four.

**I want you to remember one sentence: having seen someone on video does not mean it is safe.**

That is a blind spot for many people. They think text can be faked but video must be real. But understand this: **one photograph is enough to put anyone in front of a camera.**

And that technology needs no professional equipment. It needs a clear frontal photo — and those are all over social media.

**So we need to update an old safety instinct.** The old instinct was that seeing someone on video meant you were safe. That instinct has expired.

**So what do you do?** The question is not whether they look right. It is whether the situation makes sense.

An example. If a perfect match you met three days ago starts talking about investments and transfers, **then however real the video looks, the situation itself does not make sense.** That is the principle from session two: do not only look for what is fake. Ask whether this makes sense.

> **Aside** The amount, about a hundred and thirty thousand, and the sentence, three years suspended for four, are concrete teaching material. You can ask: does that sentence seem heavy or light? Then guide them to consider whether punishment is for retribution or deterrence. That discussion leads into the law section in session three.

## Slide 11 · Mainland case two: Grandma Liu and the call from her grandson

One last case. Grandma Liu and a call from her grandson.

The fraudster synthesised her grandson's voice, claimed he was in trouble and needed money urgently, and took thirty thousand.

I want to close session one with this case, because it carries the most important observation: **the deepest deepfake frauds do not deceive the eyes. They deceive the feelings.**

Look back at the four cases: a voice in distress, an organised gang, a romance video call, a grandson calling. **Notice that the most effective is not the most technically sophisticated. It is the one that engages emotion most.**

**And that is why the more emotionally engaged you are, the more you need to slow down and verify from several directions.**

Here is a practical self-check: **whenever a message makes your heart race and makes you want to act immediately, that is the moment to start the four steps.**

It is simple and it works. Because when your heart is racing you will not remember to verify. So you need a pre-set trigger. Racing heart is the easiest trigger to recognise.

**That is session one.** We looked at four cases and reached one conclusion: the naked eye is unreliable; a process is reliable.

**That conclusion is where session two begins.** After the break we learn stop, look, check, find, and see where the flaws usually hide.

> **Teaching note** Session one is about feeling. Do not rush to definitions. After the warm-up, land one sentence — the naked eye is unreliable, a process is reliable — and use it to set up session two. If students seem badly shaken by the cases, add: these cases are here so we can protect ourselves and the people around us, not to frighten you.

# Session 2 · Technical principles and identification (35 min · 12–21)

## Slide 12 · Session 2 divider

Session two. Thirty-five minutes, the densest section of the day.

**One important thing first: this is not about teaching you to use tools. It is about teaching you to understand where the flaws are.**

A lot of people watch deepfake detection tutorials online and learn to look at the eyes, the teeth, the background. Those are useful, but they are not enough, because you need to know **why** those places show flaws.

**Without the reason, you are just memorising. And when the technology advances, what you memorised stops working.**

So we start with the principles: how generative adversarial networks work. Then four common types of deepfake. Then the four-step method.

**I promise: this will not be very technical. No mathematics, no architecture. Only why something happens.**

> **Aside** Watch the pace. Keep the technical principle on slide 13 brisk and give more time to the four-step method on slides 15 to 20 and the exercise on slide 21, because those are what students will actually use.

## Slide 13 · The heart of deepfake: generative adversarial networks (GAN)

Here is the core technology. It is called a GAN, a generative adversarial network.

No mathematics. An analogy: **two AIs competing, one forging, one detecting.**

**The first role, the generator.** It forges. It keeps generating fake images and voices, aiming to fool the discriminator. **Every time it is caught, it adjusts and improves.**

**The second role, the discriminator.** It detects. It tries to tell real from fake and feeds back the clues about what gave it away.

**Their relationship is adversarial.** The better the generator gets, the stricter the discriminator becomes; the stricter the discriminator, the more the generator must improve.

**Think of it as a duel between a counterfeiter and a banknote examiner.** Every time the examiner spots a flaw, the counterfeiter fixes it. Eventually the examiner cannot tell.

**And that is the conclusion: the two train each other, the fakes get better, and the flaws become harder and harder to see with the naked eye.**

You might ask: if we know how it works, can we find the flaws? **Partly.** Because in learning, the generator has systematic weaknesses — it may fail to learn a certain kind of lighting or texture.

**But you should not depend on those.** Every advance patches them. The flaw you can spot today may be gone next year.

**So our strategy should not be spotting flaws. It should be using a process**, because a process does not become obsolete.

> **Teaching note** Demonstrate the adversarial idea with a small game: two students, one drawing a circle, the other identifying it as hand-drawn. The drawer improves each time they are caught. After three rounds students feel the mechanism themselves. Simple, but effective.

## Slide 14 · Four common types of deepfake

Four types. I will cover each and its detection difficulty.

**First, face swap.** Putting one person's face onto another person's video. **This is the most common video fraud technique.** The difficulty is that the body and movements are real, so the whole thing feels natural.

**Second, voice cloning.** A few seconds of recording is enough to imitate someone's voice and manner. **This is the hardest to detect by eye — or rather by ear — because sound has no image quality to inspect.** And phone audio is degraded anyway, which masks the synthetic traces.

**Third, motion synthesis.** Making a person in a photo perform movements and expressions they never made. The feature is that you do not need a video. A single photo is enough.

**Fourth, full synthesis.** The entire person and scene are generated from nothing. This person never existed. This is the hardest to verify, because the step of finding the person and asking them cannot be done. There is no person.

**And here is a very important sentence, which I will say slowly.**

**This technology creates value in film, education and games. The problem is not the technology. It is whether the user is honest and respects others.**

That is my core position today. Deepfake technology is not inherently bad — cinema uses it for effects, education to recreate historical figures, games to create characters. **What is bad is using it to deceive and to harm.**

**This position matters, because it decides our attitude.** We should not oppose the technology. We should require users to be honest. That is the foundation of the transparency principle in session three.

> **Likely question** "So should deepfake technology be banned?" There is no simple answer. Banning a technology is usually very hard, because it has legitimate uses, and it can stifle innovation. So the direction is regulating use rather than banning technology. That is the thinking behind the mainland's deep synthesis regulations.

## Slide 15 · Overview: stop, look, check, find

Now the most important method today: the four-step method.

**Stop, look, check, find.**

**Stop:** pause first. **Urgency is itself a warning sign.**

**Look:** examine facial lighting and shadows; listen to the audio and background.

**Check:** check the source, the date, the origin.

**Find:** use tools to verify, or verify directly with the person.

**And here is the line I most want you to notice: this process is deliberately designed to slow you down.**

It is not a set of quick judgement techniques. Quite the opposite — it is a mechanism that forces you to slow down. Because as we said in session one, your weakness is not that you cannot see. It is that you are too fast and too easily moved.

**So when do you trigger these four steps? As I said before: whenever a message makes your heart race and makes you want to act immediately.**

That is your trigger. You do not need to do all four every time; that would be exhausting. You only need to start when your heart races.

**I suggest you decide now where you will keep this trigger.** Because a method with no trigger will never be used.

> **Teaching note** This is the core of session two. Write the four steps on the board, and as you cover each one, point at that word. The visual repetition is what makes it stick.

## Slide 16 · Stop: do not believe yet, and do not rush to share

First step: stop.

Which four kinds of message most need the brakes?

**First, ones that rush you.** Limited time, right now, do not tell your family. Why are these warnings? Because their function is **to remove your verification time.** A legitimate request does not forbid you from thinking.

**Second, ones that frighten you.** Impersonating police, prosecutors, banks. These exploit fear. **And fear makes people want to solve the problem immediately**, so they do not verify.

**Third, ones that sweeten you.** A big prize, an incredible price. The feeling that this is too good and will not last also removes your thinking time.

**Fourth, ones that make you cry.** A relative in trouble. That is the Grandma Liu case.

**Notice what all four share.** Every one of them **removes your time to verify.**

**So the value of stop is this: stopping for thirty seconds alone blocks a great deal of risk.**

Thirty seconds. Not long. You only need to stop for thirty seconds and ask: why is this so urgent?

**That is a very good self-check question.** If the other party is genuine, they will not mind you taking thirty seconds. If they are not, those thirty seconds may save you a great deal of money.

> **Teaching note** Act out the four kinds of message. Play the fraudster, using an urgent tone: your son is in trouble, transfer the money now, do not tell anyone. Students feel the force of urgency. Then demonstrate hanging up. The dramatisation lodges in memory.

## Slide 17 · Look part one: flaws on the face

Second step: look. Two slides, starting with the face.

**First observation point: edges and outlines.**

Look at the boundary between face and hair, and the ears. Is it blurred, jittery, or unnaturally coloured?

Why those places? Because synthesising boundaries is where the technology makes the most mistakes. The centre of a face is relatively easy. The seam with the background — hairline, ear outline — is much harder.

**Second observation point: eyes and blinking.**

Is the gaze blank? Is the blink rate abnormal? Is the reflection in the glasses consistent?

Blinking is a good indicator, because video synthesis sometimes cannot handle the natural rhythm. Glasses reflections are even better, because the two lenses should reflect consistently, and a synthesis may not manage that.

**Third observation point: teeth and mouth shapes.**

Does the mouth match the speech? Are the teeth a blur?

Teeth are a classic flaw, because the inside of the mouth is structurally complex and poor synthesis turns it into a smear.

**But here is a very important sentence: these clues disappear as the technology advances. Reading the face is only the first step.**

You should not treat face-reading as the core method, because generators improve daily. The flaw you see today may be patched next year.

**So look is a supporting step. The real core is check and find.**

> **Likely question** "So if I cannot see a flaw, is it real?" No. Not seeing a flaw does not mean it is real. It means your naked eye cannot see one. That is why we need four steps rather than one.

## Slide 18 · Look part two: flaws in sound and background

Continuing look. Now sound and background.

**Listening first.**

**First, is the tone flat?** People's voices rise and fall. Synthetic voices are sometimes too even, because they are generated syllable by syllable and may not capture natural emotional variation.

**Second, is there a lack of breathing and pauses?** Real speech pauses, breathes, and has fillers like um and er. If a clip has none of that, it may be synthetic.

**Third, is there electronic noise or splicing in the background?** Synthetic audio sometimes has a faint electronic quality, or unnatural seams between sentences.

**Now watching the background.**

**Is the lighting direction consistent?** If the face is lit but the background light comes from the other side, something is wrong.

**Are the shadows sensible?** Synthesis sometimes cannot handle complex shadows.

**Are object edges consistent?** Does the background distort or flicker suddenly?

**But here is a more important principle. Please remember this sentence:**

**Do not only look for what is fake. Ask whether this makes sense.**

That sentence is the culmination of the look step.

Why? Because hunting flaws is a game you will lose — the technology advances and the flaws vanish. But asking whether something makes sense is a game you win, because the purpose of a fraud is always the same: get you to transfer money or hand over information.

**An example.** A video with no visible flaw. But the content is a friend telling you to transfer money immediately to a stranger's account. **However real the video, transferring money to a stranger's account does not make sense.**

**So your centre of gravity should move from the technical level to the logical level.** On the technical level you may lose. On the logical level you always have the advantage, because you know how to ask why.

> **Teaching note** This principle is the most important line in the section. Pause after it, then ask: if your mother's video asked you to transfer money to an account you had never heard of, what would you do? They will say they would phone her. At that moment they understand that asking whether it makes sense is more effective than judging real or fake.

## Slide 19 · Check: trace the source

Third step: check. Here you move from passive observation to active verification.

**Three questions.**

**First: who published it?** Where did this come from? A reputable outlet, or an anonymous account?

**Second: when?** When is this content from? **This one matters especially, because a lot of news is old footage recycled.** A video from 2020 with a 2026 headline becomes fake news.

**Third: why should I believe it?** What is the publisher's motive? What do they want you to do? **This is the most powerful question, because it goes straight to purpose.**

**Then cross-reference.**

Check other credible outlets or official channels for the same event. If a major story has only one source, and that source is an anonymous social media account, that is suspicious.

**Two useful techniques.**

**First, feed a video screenshot into image search.** This finds where the image came from and when. A lot of fake news is exposed at this step, because you find the original and discover it is years old.

**Second, search the keywords plus the word fact-check.** Often you are not the first person to be suspicious. A fact-checking organisation may have done the work.

**Notice that this step costs very little — perhaps two minutes.** But it is highly effective, because fraudsters rarely expect you to check.

> **Aside** Demonstrate reverse image search live. Take an old news photo, search it, and show how you find the original source and date. When students see it takes two minutes, the method becomes plausible to them.

## Slide 20 · Find: use tools, and ask the real person

Fourth step: find. This is gathering evidence and verifying directly.

**Four tools.**

**First, official fact-checking.** Most places have official or semi-official verification platforms.

**Second, reverse search.** The image and video search from the last slide.

**Third, verify directly.** **Call the person back on a number you already knew.** That line from session one appears again here, because it is the core action of the whole process.

**Fourth, report it.** You can report to the police, or to the anti-fraud coordination centre.

**And of the four, the most important is the third: reach the person, and confirm on another channel.**

Why? **Because that is precisely what fraudsters are most afraid you will do.**

Think about their design. They want you to act immediately and not tell anyone. What do they fear? You going to the person to confirm.

**So the essence of the fourth step is: break the fraudster's design.** They want you isolated and urgent. You want connected and slow.

**I especially want to stress the idea of another channel.** Do not confirm on the same channel. If they contacted you on WhatsApp, do not ask back on WhatsApp, because that account may itself have been taken over. Use a phone call, or another channel.

**This principle is called switching channels to confirm.** It is very simple and very effective.

> **Teaching note** Use a concrete example for switching channels: if a friend's WhatsApp says they urgently need to borrow money, what do you do? Most will say they would ask back on WhatsApp. Point out that is wrong, because the account may be compromised. The correct action is to phone.

## Slide 21 · Classroom exercise: detectives on duty

Now the exercise. This closes session two.

**Work in groups of four or five. Each group picks a leader and a recorder.**

**Four steps: form groups, take a case, analyse with the four steps, present.**

Each group gets material — a video, a screenshot, or an audio clip. **Your group applies stop, look, check, find in order, then presents your judgement.**

**When you present, cover two things: your conclusion, and the single clue you found most convincing.**

**And I want to stress this: the point is not guessing right. It is explaining clearly how you judged. The process matters more than the answer.**

Why? Because the exercise is not testing your eye. It is training your process. **If you guess right but cannot explain why, that was luck. If you guess wrong but can explain your reasoning clearly, you have learned something.**

This matters, because in the real world you will not be told the answer immediately. All you can control is whether your process was rigorous.

**Keep each presentation to ninety seconds.** I will follow up using the four steps, to reinforce the process.

> **Ops note** Prepare three or four sets of material before the session, ranging in difficulty. **Include both real and fake**, because if everything is fake students conclude the activity is about hunting fakes and stop applying the four steps properly. Keep each presentation to ninety seconds and follow up with the four steps.

# Break · 5 minutes (slide 22)

## Slide 22 · A short break

Let's take five minutes.

When we return we look at harder questions. **When AI can imitate anyone, how should law and ethics respond?**

# Session 3 · Law and ethics (35 min · 23–29)

## Slide 23 · Session 3 divider

Session three.

Here we take on a harder question: **when AI can imitate anyone, how should law and ethics respond?**

Why harder? Because in sessions one and two we dealt with technical problems — how to identify, how to verify. Technical problems have a property: **if you do it right, there is an answer.**

Law and ethics are different. **The same act may be lawful but unethical.** It may even be lawful and widely considered acceptable — and still you know something is wrong.

**So we need two layers of tools.** Law is the minimum standard: cross it and you are punished. Ethics is the higher standard: nobody punishes you, but you know what you ought to do.

We will look at Hong Kong law, then mainland regulation, then discuss three real scenarios, and finally I will give you three principles.

**The aim is not that you memorise legal provisions. It is that you can make a decision in the grey areas the law does not reach.**

> **Teaching note** The densest section. Use scenario first, provision second. Avoid reading provisions aloud — they are there to support judgement, not to test memory.

## Slide 24 · Hong Kong: how does current law address deepfakes?

Starting with law.

A fact that may surprise you: **Hong Kong does not currently have a dedicated deepfake law.**

But let me add immediately, so you do not misunderstand: **no dedicated law does not mean no one is accountable.** Fraud by falsification can be prosecuted under existing legislation.

Four relevant provisions or bodies.

**First, the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance.** Using another person's image or voice without consent may breach privacy protections. This applies when someone uses your face or voice to generate content without your agreement.

**Second, section 16A of the Theft Ordinance — fraud.** Obtaining benefit by deception, for example impersonating someone with a deepfake.

**Third, section 17 of the Theft Ordinance — obtaining property by deception.** Obtaining money or property by false representation is a criminal offence. The difference from 16A is that 16A covers benefit and 17 covers property.

**Fourth, the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data.** The Commissioner has published guidance to help organisations and individuals reduce deepfake risk. That is guidance rather than legislation, but it represents the official position and advice.

**Why go through them one by one? Because I want you to understand something: the law may lag technology, but that does not mean there is a legal vacuum.**

Technology raises new problems, but the act of deceiving people has always been covered. A deepfake is a new method, not a new crime.

**The practical implication: if you are defrauded, you can report it and seek redress. You should not feel there is nowhere to turn because there is no dedicated provision.**

> **Teaching note** There is a lot of legal content, so use scenario first. Give a scenario — someone made a video using your face without consent — and ask whether that is unlawful. Then bring in the provision. That order gives the law meaning rather than turning it into a recitation.

## Slide 25 · What can schools and parents do?

Beyond law, a more important layer: **law is the last line of defence. Education and institutions are the first.**

Why? Because law operates after the fact. You were defrauded, so you report it. Education operates beforehand, so that you are not defrauded at all.

Three directions.

**First, set norms.** Schools establish codes of conduct: no making or distributing face-swap images or synthesised voices without consent. **The purpose of such a code is not only to prohibit but to build consensus**, so everyone knows where the line is.

**Second, education first.** Teach identification. Cultivate empathy. Do not only teach prohibition.

Let me expand on that. **If you only teach prohibition, students learn that this is not allowed. They do not learn why.** And when they leave school and the environment changes, they will do it, because what they learned was a rule, not a judgement.

**If you teach empathy, students learn to ask what they would want if they were the subject.** That judgement stays with them for life.

**Third, routes to help.** Know about the Privacy Commissioner, the police, and school counselling. **This matters, because many victims do not seek help simply because they do not know where to go.**

**And finally, one thing to the students here: being synthesised or impersonated is not your fault.**

I want to say that slowly. Because victims often blame themselves — was it because I posted too many photos, was I too careless. **That self-blame is mistaken.** The person who did wrong is the one who used your face to harm someone, not you.

**If this happens to you, do two things: tell a trusted adult immediately, and preserve the evidence.**

Preserving evidence matters, because many people immediately delete the video or block the account — and in doing so destroy the evidence. **Screenshot first, save the links, and then deal with it.**

> **Teaching note** Being synthesised is not your fault is a sensitive but necessary message. **There may be a student in the room who has been a victim.** Use a supportive tone, not a pitying one. That distinction matters.

## Slide 26 · The mainland: the deep synthesis regulations

Let's look at the mainland approach. It is worth studying, because it is among the world's earliest dedicated regulation of deep synthesis.

**The Deep Synthesis Management Provisions, in force from 10 January 2023.**

Four key points.

**First, labelling requirements.** Synthesised content must be clearly marked. That is the central requirement.

**Second, consent.** Using biometric information such as faces and voices requires prior consent.

**Third, platform responsibility.** Platforms must establish review, complaint and handling mechanisms. **This matters because it extends responsibility from the individual to the platform.**

**Fourth, prohibited uses.** Manufacturing or spreading rumours, or infringing others' rights, is prohibited.

**And the spirit of it is one sentence: you may synthesise, but people must know, and you must not use it to harm.**

Notice the structure. It is not a prohibition on synthesis. It is permission with two conditions.

**That approach is worth learning from.** It acknowledges legitimate uses — film, education, entertainment — while setting boundaries. It is far more pragmatic, and more workable, than an outright ban.

**And the labelling requirement is a clever design.** It does not require judging whether content is harmful. It only requires judging whether content is synthesised. The former is highly subjective; the latter is objective. **Turning a subjective question into an objective one is good regulatory design.**

> **Aside** Connect labelling to the transparency principle from session two. The legal requirement and the ethical principle point the same way: do not pass off what is fake as what is real.

## Slide 27 · The mainland: labelling rules and criminal liability

This extends the last slide, and covers the newest development.

**The Measures for Labelling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, effective 1 September 2025, is a comprehensive new labelling regime.**

It requires AI-generated text, images, audio and video to carry clear labels. **Note the word comprehensive — not only video, but text as well.**

**And malicious misuse — fraud, defamation, creating indecent imagery — may constitute a criminal offence.**

**So the trend is: not banning AI, but requiring transparency and traceability.**

Let me unpack those two words. Transparency means you must tell people this was AI-generated. Traceability means that if something goes wrong, it must be possible to find who generated it.

**Both directions answer the problems we have discussed today.**

Think about it. If every fraud from session one carried a label saying AI-generated, could the fraud still work? No, because the victim would see it immediately.

**So labelling solves the problem at the source.** It does not require you to identify or verify. It simply tells you.

**But it has a limit: when labelling is voluntary, fraudsters will not label.** So it needs legal compulsion and platform enforcement. That is why platform responsibility matters so much.

> **Likely question** "What if the fraudster uses tools from another country?" A very practical question. That is why international cooperation matters, and why your own ability to identify remains the last line of defence. Law and technology both have limits, so your personal judgement cannot be replaced.

## Slide 28 · Ethics discussion: three scenarios you will meet

Now from law to ethics.

**Why ethics? Because where the law reaches we use legal provisions, and where it does not we rely on judgement.**

Three scenarios. All three are things you will actually encounter, not hypotheticals.

**Scenario one: a classmate's joke.**

A classmate makes a face-swap meme of you and the whole class finds it funny. What do you do?

Notice the difficulty: **the people around you think it is fine.** If you object you may be told you cannot take a joke, that you are too serious. That is the hardest part — you are resisting not only the person who did it but the group.

**Scenario two: online dating.**

Someone claims to be a particular person and sends you a video. How do you verify? Who do you tell?

The difficulty: **you are already emotionally invested.** Verifying means admitting you may have been deceived. That is a hard psychological threshold.

**Scenario three: the limits of AI creation.**

Is it acceptable to use an AI-generated celebrity voice for an assignment or a video? Where is the line?

The difficulty: **there is no obvious victim.** The celebrity may never know. But does that make it acceptable?

**For discussion: each group picks one, and speaks to the possible choices and consequences from three perspectives — the person involved, the bystander, and the platform.**

Why three perspectives? Because in the same scenario, the three roles carry different responsibilities. **The person involved must protect themselves. The bystander must decide whether to speak up. The platform must decide whether to intervene.** Together, the three give you the whole picture.

> **Teaching note** Give this discussion real time, at least eight to ten minutes. **Do not supply an answer.** If students ask for the correct answer, say: there is no standard answer to these three, but your answer reflects your principles, and I will give you three principles on the next slide. That makes them look forward to it.

## Slide 29 · Three principles for deciding in grey areas

How do you decide in a grey area? Three principles.

**First, informed consent.**

Before using another person's face, voice or identity, ask: **did they agree?**

This is the simplest and the most powerful, because it shifts the question from whether it is technically possible to whether they agreed. **Being able to do something and being right to do it are completely different things.**

**Second, minimal harm.**

Even if it is technically possible, think carefully: **will this harm anyone?**

This handles the no obvious victim case. Ask yourself: if this became public, who would be uncomfortable? Often you will find someone you had overlooked.

**Third, transparency and honesty.**

AI-generated content should be clearly labelled. Do not pass it off as real.

This answers the labelling requirement directly. **Honesty is not only about not lying. It also means not letting people be misled.**

**And one sentence to sum up the three: the technology is neutral, but the choice is not.**

Technology has no inherent good or evil. The same deepfake technology can make film effects or defraud people. **The difference is not the technology. It is the choice.**

**Finally, the simplest self-test: if I were the subject, how would I want others to act?**

This requires no legal knowledge and no technical knowledge. It requires empathy. And that is why education matters more than law — **law tells you what you must not do; empathy tells you what you should do.**

> **Aside** This closes session three. After the three principles, go back to the three scenarios and ask: using these principles, what would you choose? That connects the abstract principles to the concrete cases.

# Session 4 · Summary and action (15 min · 30–33)

## Slide 30 · Session 4 divider

The last session. Fifteen minutes.

We started with a quiz, looked at four real cases, learned about generative adversarial networks and four kinds of deepfake, practised the four-step method, and discussed law and ethics. Now we close.

**What I want you to take home tonight is not a pile of knowledge. It is a method, a kind of courage, and a clearer attitude to trust.**

A method is the four steps. Courage is daring to verify and daring to ask for help. A clearer attitude is understanding that trust is not a binary choice between believing and not believing — it is a capacity you can calibrate.

> **Teaching note** Fifteen minutes, so divide it: slide 31 five minutes, slide 32 five minutes, slide 33 five minutes. Do not overrun, because the closing message needs to be delivered unhurried.

## Slide 31 · Three things learned today

Three things.

**First, the phenomenon: deepfakes are already around us.**

AI face swapping and voice cloning are being used to defraud. The numbers we saw today — two thousand and twenty, one point seven times, seventeen million, thirty-four million — are not forecasts. They have happened.

**Second, the principle: generative adversarial networks make faces and voices ever more real.**

The flaws will disappear. The process will not.

That is the most important line today. You cannot put your hope in being able to spot flaws, because the flaws disappear. You can only put your hope in having a process, because the process does not.

**Third, the method: use the deliberately slowed four steps, plus legal awareness and ethical judgement, to make responsible choices.**

**And the line I most want you to remember:**

**Do not refuse to believe anything because you fear being deceived. Learn to believe with discipline, because you know how to verify.**

That is the core of the whole class. Think again about what I said at the start: if you leave this class unable to trust anything, I have failed.

**Because believing nothing and believing everything are equally dangerous.** Someone who believes everything gets defrauded. Someone who believes nothing loses every relationship — they trust no friend, no news, no information. **Both extremes fail.**

**The path between them is believing with discipline.** You believe, but you verify. You do not close yourself off because you were deceived once, and you do not abandon verification because people deserve trust.

> **Teaching note** Deliver the key line slowly. Pause for two or three seconds afterwards to let it settle. The pause gives the sentence weight.

## Slide 32 · Five things you can do from today

Five concrete actions. I ask that you leave remembering at least two.

**First, slow down one step.** When you see a message that rushes or threatens you, stop for thirty seconds.

Thirty seconds. The cost is negligible and the effect is large. You only need to ask: why is this so urgent?

**Second, switch channels.** If a relative or friend sends a distress message, hang up first, then call them back on another number.

This is the most practical line today. **Remember the words another number. That is the key.**

**Third, check the source.** Use reverse image search and keywords to find the original origin and date.

Two minutes. And often one search shows it is old footage recycled.

**Fourth, do not spread it.** Unverified images or audio: do not forward them, do not use them as material.

This one is about your own behaviour. **Have you ever forwarded something that seemed outrageous?** If so, you have already taken part in spreading it. And spreading false material harms people too.

**Fifth, dare to ask for help.** If you are impersonated or defrauded, tell a trusted adult immediately and report it.

**I want to stress the fifth: asking for help is not weakness.** Many people do not ask because they feel ashamed. But understand that making you feel ashamed is part of the fraudster's design — it stops you speaking. **The sooner you ask for help, the smaller the loss, and you may help others, because your report tells law enforcement about the method.**

**And one final reminder: protecting your own information security and respecting others' likeness and voice are the same thing.**

Why the same thing? Because both come down to **respect for people.** You respect your own privacy, so you protect your data. You respect others' privacy, so you do not misuse their likeness. One value, two directions.

> **Teaching note** Write these five on the board or print them as a handout, because these are what students will actually retain and use. If you hand out only one thing, hand out these five.

## Slide 33 · Closing

Last slide.

One sentence to close.

**"In the AI era, the real skill is not doubting everything. It is knowing how to believe with discipline."**

I opened today with a question, and I want to return to it. I said I was not here to teach you to trust nothing. Do you remember why?

Because doubting everything is the easy answer. If you believe nothing you cannot be hurt — but you also cannot connect with anyone. **That is not a life. It is a fortification.**

**Believing with discipline is a much harder answer.** It asks you to stay open and stay alert. To trust people and to verify. To accept that you may be wrong, and not to let that stop you trying.

**That is the capacity I wanted to give you today.**

You now have the identification tools — stop, look, check, find. You have basic legal awareness — knowing that no dedicated law does not mean no accountability. And you have three principles in mind: informed consent, minimal harm, transparency and honesty.

**Now it is your turn, in your own life, to be the person willing to stop and verify.**

One last thing. The cases today are not about how clever bad people are. They are about how vulnerable good people are. The victims were not foolish. They were trusting. And trust is the most precious thing a society has.

**So what we did today is not only protecting ourselves. It is protecting trust itself.**

**When you pause for thirty seconds, when you make one more phone call to confirm, when you do not forward an unverified video — you have not only protected yourself. You have spared the trust in this society one more injury.**

Thank you. Questions — let's talk.

> **Teaching note** This closes the whole class. Deliver it slowly, especially the last part about protecting trust itself. That is the emotional peak. Afterwards, do not immediately ask for questions. Pause for three seconds and let the room be quiet. That pause deepens the message.

# Appendix D · Common misconceptions

## Misconception 1: "I can tell which one is fake."

Research consistently shows human accuracy is only slightly above chance. Confidence is not accuracy.

## Misconception 2: "Seeing someone on video means it is safe."

One clear photo is enough. This instinct has expired.

## Misconception 3: "Deepfakes are inherently bad."

Film, education and games use them legitimately. The problem is dishonest use, not the technology.

## Misconception 4: "I will not be a target."

Victims are chosen for their circumstances, not their profile. Anyone with a relative who might panic is a target.

## Misconception 5: "I know about technology, so I will not fall for it."

Technical knowledge does not protect against emotional hijacking. That is the attack vector.

## Misconception 6: "AI-generated content is always labelled."

Labelling is not universal, and fraudsters do not label voluntarily.

## Misconception 7: "The law does not cover deepfakes."

There is no dedicated law, but fraud and privacy provisions apply. No dedicated provision does not mean no accountability.

## Misconception 8: "Just delete the fake video."

Deleting destroys your evidence. Screenshot and save links first.

## Misconception 9: "Being defrauded means I am stupid."

Being defrauded means your trust was exploited. The fault lies with the fraudster.

## Misconception 10: "Forwarding is harmless."

Forwarding false material causes harm and can carry liability.

## Misconception 11: "Using a celebrity's voice for an assignment is fine."

No consent, no legitimate purpose in most cases. The absence of an obvious victim does not make it acceptable.

## Misconception 12: "Detection tools can be trusted completely."

They produce both false positives and false negatives. Use them as one input, never as the verdict.

## Misconception 13: "This only affects secondary students."

Victims span all ages. Older people are disproportionately targeted by voice fraud.

## Misconception 14: "After this class I will not be defrauded."

You will be better prepared. Nobody is immune.

# Appendix E · Discussion guides for the three scenarios

## Scenario one · A classmate's joke

**Key question:** what does consent mean between friends?

**Guide:** ask whether laughter counts as consent. Ask what the difference is between a joke and a joke that targets someone. The point is not that humour is forbidden, but that the target's feeling decides whether it landed.

**Expected landing:** a shared joke among friends who both enjoy it is one thing. A meme made of someone who did not agree, and circulated to a group, is another.

## Scenario two · Online dating

**Key question:** how much does emotional investment damage your judgement?

**Guide:** ask what the student would need to see to become suspicious, and whether they would tell a friend. The second question is often the harder one.

**Expected landing:** verifying is not an accusation. It is what you do for anyone, including people you like.

## Scenario three · The limits of AI creation

**Key question:** does the absence of a victim mean no harm?

**Guide:** ask who might be harmed even if the celebrity never finds out. Consider the audience who believes it is real, and consider the norm it establishes.

**Expected landing:** the absence of an obvious victim is not the absence of harm. It is a sign to look harder.

# Appendix F · Rehearsal: likely questions and answers

## Scene one · "So should I trust nothing?"

**You could answer:**

"If you trust nothing, you lose everyone. That is not protection. That is isolation.

The skill is not refusing to believe. It is **believing with a step of verification built in.** You still trust your friends. You just make one extra phone call before you transfer money.

**You are not becoming suspicious. You are becoming slower and more deliberate in the moments that matter.**"

**Why answer this way:** this is the central fear of the session. If unanswered, students leave with the wrong takeaway.

## Scene two · "My mother keeps getting these calls. What do I do?"

**You could answer:**

"Teach her one rule and drill it: hang up, then call back on a number she already has saved.

Not a lecture on deepfake technology. One rule, practised. Ask her to say it back to you.

**And tell her this: no real family member will be angry that you hung up to check.** If someone on the phone is angry that you want to verify, that is the strongest signal you will get."

**Why answer this way:** it gives them something they can actually do tonight, which is what the question is really asking for.

## Scene three · "My photo was made into a meme. What should I do?"

**You could answer:**

"First, know this: **it is not your fault.** You did not do anything wrong by having a face.

Second, the practical steps. If you are at school, tell a teacher or counsellor. Preserve the evidence — screenshot and save the link before anything is deleted. If it is being used to extort or defame you, that may be a criminal matter, so report it.

**And if it is a classmate, you are allowed to say it is not funny.** That can be hard, because the group may push back. But your discomfort is legitimate information."

**Why answer this way:** there may be a real victim in the room. Lead with the reassurance, then give concrete steps.

## Scene four · "If I already transferred the money, is there anything I can do?"

**You could answer:**

"Yes, and speed matters more than embarrassment.

**Report immediately.** Contact the police, and the anti-fraud coordination centre. The faster a transfer is reported, the better the chance it can be traced or stopped. Every hour reduces the chance.

Do not spend time feeling ashamed first. That delay is exactly what the fraudster is counting on."

**Why answer this way:** shame is the main reason people delay, and the delay is what costs them.

## Scene five · "Do AI detection tools work?"

**You could answer:**

"Partly, and nothing like reliably.

They produce both false positives, calling real content fake, and false negatives, missing fake content. And as generators improve, the detection gap widens.

**So use them as one input, never as the verdict.** A tool saying probably fake is a reason to check further, not a conclusion. The four-step method does not depend on any tool working."

**Why answer this way:** students want a technical shortcut. Being clear that none exists redirects them to the process.

## Scene six · "Should schools just ban phones?"

**You could answer:**

"Banning a phone addresses the tool. It does not address the judgement.

And it does not work well, because the phone is not where the problem starts. The problem starts with a voice on a call saying something is urgent.

**What works is what we practised today:** a process a student can run under pressure. That is portable. A ban is not."

**Why answer this way:** it reframes from prohibition to capability, matching the whole session's argument.

# Appendix J · Classroom situations

## Situation one · A student discloses being a victim

**What to do:** stop the exercise. Take it aside as a pastoral matter. Reassure them that it is not their fault, tell them you will help them report it, and follow your school's safeguarding procedure. Do not discuss it in front of the class.

## Situation two · A student claims they can always spot fakes

**What to do:** do not argue. Show the warm-up results and the research finding, then ask them to run the four steps on a hard example. Let the exercise do the work.

## Situation three · The room is distressed by the cases

**What to do:** name it. Say the purpose of the cases is protection, not fear. Then move to the practical actions, which restore a sense of control.

## Situation four · Running badly over time

**What to do:** compress the case details in session one to summaries, keep the warm-up and the exercise, and deliver session three as the three principles plus one scenario rather than all three.

## Situation five · The exercise material is too easy

**What to do:** every group finishes early and confidently. Have spare harder material ready, and add the four-step follow-up questions. The point of the exercise is the reasoning, not the verdict, so harder material produces better discussion.

## Situation six · Nobody will present

**What to do:** ask only for the single most convincing clue, not the full presentation. That is a much smaller ask, and once one group speaks others follow.
